Efficacy of Electrical Stimulation on Nerve
Fiber Growth in Small Fiber Neuropathy
Ahmet Z. Burakgazi, MD*†
Abstract
Objectives:
To define whether electrical nerve stimulation
(ENS) therapy would promote intraepidermal
nerve growth and nerve regeneration in patients
with small fiber neuropathy (SFN).

Methods:
This was a prospective study conducted on 8
subjects with previously diagnosed SFN. Nerve
conduction testing, punch biopsies, and clinical
examinations with a calculation of revised total
neuropathy score were conducted on subjects
before beginning ENS therapy and at 30 and 60 days
after the start of ENS therapy.
Results:
Clinical examination findings and intraepidermal
nerve fiber density measurements on day 30 and
day 60 did not show statistically significant changes in
the treated group compared with the untreated group.
Conclusions:
Despite the success of previous animal studies, no
meaningful nerve growth and regeneration in SFN
was demonstrated with ENS therapy in this study.
Studies of larger subject larger populations with
longer duration of ENS treatment are warranted to
confirm our findings.
Key Words: electrical nerve stimulation, small fiber
neuropathy

INTRODUCTION
Peripheral neuropathy (PN) is one of
the most common neurological disorders in
the United States.1 Approximately 15–20 million people in the United States suffer from
some type of PN.2 There are different types
of PN. Small fiber neuropathy (SFN) is a common form of PN that primarily or exclusively
affects small somatic and autonomic fibers.3

The clinical symptoms and physical
examination findings of SFN are well
defined.4–7 Novel diagnostic tests such as
skin biopsy and autonomic nerve function
tests have been developed over the past 2
decades to aid in diagnosis. Several
SFN-associated conditions such as glucose
dysmetabolism, connective tissue diseases,
sarcoidosis, hypothyroidism, vitamin B12
deficiency, paraproteinemia, HIV infection,
celiac disease, neurotoxic drug, and paraneoplastic syndrome have been described
as well.5,6 However, in approximately 50%
of these cases, no underlying etiological
cause can be determined.6,7
Management of SFN includes identifying and treating the underlying cause, lifestyle modification, and pain management
with antidepressants, anticonvulsants, topical
anesthetics, opioids, and opioid agonists.8
Treatment of the underlying cause of SFN
may slow or stop the progression of the disease; however, currently available medications only alleviate SFN-related symptoms
without disrupting the disease progression,9
and patients treated with these medications
may suffer undesirable side effects. Thus, the
need for alternative treatment approaches to
stop or slow down the progression of SFN
remains largely unmet. Since neuropathy
including SFN causes significant impact on
quality of life, productivity, and wellness of
individuals and its related complications
cause several negative social and economical
problems in society, it is important to investigate potential alternative cure/treatment options for neuropathy.

to determine safety and efficacy in the utilization of electrical stimulation for the treatment
of patients with PN. Therefore, it is paramount
that studies be conducted in human subjects
to determine whether this treatment modality
could prove safe and effective in promoting
the growth and regeneration of nerve fibers
in patients suffering from PN.
In this study, the potential regenerative
effect of electrical nerve stimulation (ENS)
therapy on peripheral nerves in subjects
diagnosed with SFN was assessed. Nerve
conduction studies were conducted to obtain
measurements of large myelinated nerve
fibers. Skin biopsies were used in measuring
small unmyelinated nerve fibers. Nerve conduction studies and skin biopsies from several time points throughout the study were
compared with determine changes in nerve
fiber growth. The hypothesis in this study
was that ENS therapy would promote intraepidermal nerve growth and nerve regeneration in patients with SFN PN.

METHODS
This was a prospective study conducted
on 9 subjects with previously diagnosed SFN.
Subjects were recruited between January
2016 and September 2016 at Carilion Clinic
in Roanoke, VA. This study was approved by
the local institutional review board, and written informed consent was obtained from each
participant before participation in the study.
Both male and female subjects between
18 and 70 years of age with a diagnosis of
idiopathic SFN were included in this study.
Owing to concerns regarding subject vulnerability, subjects younger than 18 years or
older than 70 years, as well as subjects with
an inability to consent, and pregnant or
nursing mothers were excluded from this
study. In an effort to mitigate interference
with other medical devices, subjects with
electronic implants were also excluded
from participation. Patients with significant
systemic disease including cancer, heart

disease, diabetes, vitamin deficiency, alcohol
abuse, peripheral vascular disease, abnormal
hemoglobin A1c, or previous exposure to
chemotherapeutic drugs were excluded from
the study as well since these diseases may
impact the normal growth of nerve fibers.
Nerve conduction testing, punch biopsies, and clinical examinations with a calculation of the revised Total Neuropathy Score
(rTNS)11 were conducted on subjects before
beginning ENS therapy (as a baseline) and at
30 and 60 days after the start of ENS therapy.
TNS is a validated peripheral nerve scoring
system calculating with subject symptoms,
signs, and results of quantitative testing. rTNS
is highly correlated with the full TNS (Spearman r 0.98); however, rTNS is more practical
measure than TNS because it does not
include more time-demanding measures
including reports of motor symptoms, autonomic symptoms, or a quantitative vibration
measurement.12 A trained neuromuscular
specialist conducted all rTNS examinations.
Nerve conduction studies were performed under a uniform protocol using a
Caldwell electromyography machine that
included bilateral sural and superficial sensory responses and bilateral common peroneal and tibial motor responses. An average
of 10 antidromic responses was recorded for
the sural nerve with the active electrode
placed behind the lateral malleolus and the
cathode 11 cm proximal in the calf. Supramaximal, single compound muscle action
potential responses were recorded for the
peroneal and tibial motor nerve with the
active electrode placed over the belly of the
extensor digitorium brevis and abductor
hallucis muscles, respectively. Stimulation
sites included the anterior ankle, immediately
distal to the fibular head, and the popliteal
fossa. A bar electrode was used for the
sensory response recordings. Limb temperature was maintained at 32°C at the dorsum of
the foot. The Nerve Conduction Study (NCS)
were performed by the same physician at
each time point, and an average of the
observed bilateral nerve responses was used
during data analysis.

ENS Treatment on SFN

Neuromuscular Electrical
Stimulation Therapy
ENS therapy for this study was delivered to 1 leg using an AvivaStim XP stimulator, with the contralateral leg serving as an
untreated control. Subjects selected which
leg would receive the ENS at their baseline
visit. Instructions on the use of the device for
ENS therapy were provided by a trained
neuromuscular specialist to subjects during
their baseline visit. After the training session,
baseline skin biopsy and NCS of sural, superficial peroneal, tibial motor, and peroneal
motor nerves were performed. Subjects were
provided a AvivaStim XP stimulator device to
take home and instructed to apply the ENS
therapy 3 times per week for 30 minutes per
session.
During ENS therapy, 4 electrodes of the
device were placed on calf muscles. Two
electrodes were placed on the gastrocnemius
muscle and 2 electrodes on the tibialis
anterior muscle. The AvivaStim XP stimulator
has 7 electrical stimulation programs with
different frequencies and durations. For this
protocol, the AvivaStim Program #3 was
used, which has a 50-Hz frequency setting,
contraction duration of 10 seconds, a relaxation period of 20 seconds, and a ramp up and
ramp down the duration of 1.5 seconds for
the study. The ENS therapy was applied 30 minutes per day for 3 days per week for 60 days.
Subjects were contacted periodically to
ensure compliance with the treatment protocol. Subjects returned to the clinic for
nerve conduction testing, punch biopsies,
and clinical examinations with a calculation
of an rTNS13,15,16 at 30 and 60 days after the
start of treatment.

Skin Biopsy
Subjects underwent 3-mm punch skin
punches on both limbs with the defined
location of 10 cm above the proximal malleolus at the time of the baseline visit. The
subjects underwent day 30 and day 60
biopsies. These biopsies were taken from
both limbs, approximately 1–2 cm apart from
the previous biopsy site. A total of 54 biopsies

(6 biopsies per patient) were collected during the study and sent to Johns Hopkins Cutaneous Nerve Laboratory for interpretation.
Specimens were fixed and stained
with PGP 9.5 (ubiquitin hydrolase, Chemicon). A trained and blinded technician
then assessed the intraepidermal nerve
fiber density (IENFD) as described elsewhere14 and compared with established
normative data for the laboratory.15

Statistics
Changes over time for the different
neuropathy measures were assessed through
mixed model regression for the IENFD and a
mixed-effects model for TNS and nerve
conduction measures, thus accounting for
longitudinal measurements within individuals. The measurements were presented as a
mean 6 SD. Repeated-measures analysis of
variance was used to analyze the change over
time, and Greenhouse–Geisser correction
was used when sphericity was violated. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS9.4.

RESULTS
Nine subjects were initially recruited
into the study. One participant was removed
from the study due to not following of the
study protocol. Of the 8 subjects, 6 were
women and 2 were men. The mean age was
52 6 7 years, range 44–60 years.

Neuropathy Signs
All subjects presented at the baseline,
day 30, and day 60 visits. Subjects did not
report any remarkable changes in their neuropathic symptoms. rTNS values for all patients stayed the same between visits. rTNS

30, and 3.22 6 0.83 on day 60.

DISCUSSION
Previous investigations of the effects of
electric stimulation in animal models demonstrated potential regeneration. A study conducted on the embryonic neurons of Fisher
rats found that brief electrical stimulation
immediately after transplantation altered
motor unit numbers and muscle properties
10 weeks later and demonstrated potential
long-term effects on the motor unit formation
and muscle force.16 Another study conducted
on the sciatic nerve of diabetic rats showed
that electrical stimulation of 10 mA or above
might be necessary to improve regeneration
after a dissect lesion.17 A study by Wang et al
showed that electrical stimulation successfully promoted motor nerve regeneration
selectivity regardless of end-organ connections and increased neural trophic factors in
the distal nerve stumps by 3 weeks after
nerve injury in rat models.13 In a recent study

by Willand et al, it was observed that daily
electrical stimulation in the muscles of rats
had significantly higher motor unit number
counts accompanied with significantly unregulated intramuscular levels of brain-derived
and glial cell line–derived neurotrophic factor mRNA compared to those without stimulation.18 However, in their study, the
corresponding levels of trophic factor mRNA
within the distal stump were not different
from one another, indicating that the intramuscular electrical stimulus does not modulate Schwann cell–derived trophic factor
transcription.18 There is currently no cure
available for most PN cases. Development of
new treatment modalities is paramount in
addressing this unmet medical need. Owing
to the positive effects of ENS on nerve
growth and regeneration observed in preclinical testing on laboratory animals, this
study was designed to investigate the effects
of ENS on human subjects diagnosed with
SNF. Effects of ENS were assessed using
objective measures, particular assessment of
IENFD with a skin biopsy. However, this
study did not produce meaningful regeneration in small and large peripheral nerve fibers
associated with ENS treatment.
There are several studies assessing ENS
in treating neuropathic pain. For instance,
Reverberi et al recently showed that peripheral ENS could be an effective modality in
managing severe neuropathic pain.19 Thankral et al reviewed 8 studies in which electrical
stimulation was used to treat diabetic neuropathy. Six studies evaluated SNF. Two studies
evaluated patients with both small and large
fiber neuropathy and reported significant
improvement in vibration and monofilament
testing and reduction in symptoms in
the electrical stimulation treatment group.
Six of 8 pain management studies identified
significant improvement in symptoms.20 Klassen et al21 demonstrated that high-tone external muscle stimulation improved healthrelated quality of life metrics in patients with
PN. On these studies, different scales were
used to assess the effect of ENS on pain

duration of the treatment. Use of ENS treatment in earlier stages of neuropathy may

larger subject larger populations with longer
duration of ENS treatment are warranted to
confirm our findings.
