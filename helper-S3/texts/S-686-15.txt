Perspective: This article presents a meta-analysis of
botulinum toxin-A for treating painful diabetic neuropathy. Ultimately, two high quality randomized controlled trials were meta-analyzed and show a
statistically and clinically significant improvement with
botulinum toxin-A on visual analogue pain scale. This
therapy could potentially help clinicians manage painful diabetic neuropathy.
Authors Shaheen E. Lakhan and Dominic N. Velasco
contributed equally to this work.

Methods. This article is a meta-analysis of two studies using BTX-A in the treatment of neuropathic
pain. Electronic searches of MEDLINE/PubMed,
EMBASE, and Cochrane Libraries using the terms
“botulinum neurotoxin” and “neuropathic pain”
were conducted. Only class I and class II therapeutic trials, as classified by the American Academy of
Neurology were included. The primary outcome
measured was the difference in visual analogue
scale (VAS) from pre-intervention and post-intervention after 1 month. Data were analyzed for
biases and heterogeneity following Cochrane and
PRISMA guidelines.
Results. Two studies on PDN were analyzed in the
meta-analysis showing improvement of 1.96 VAS
points (95% CI, 23.09 to 20.84; Z score 5 3.43,
P < 0.001) following treatment with BTX-A. This corresponds to clinically significant improvement of
“minimum change in pain.” The adverse effects of
infection at injection site was not statistically significant (P 5 0.49). BTX-A may be effective for PDN.
Conclusion. Tests for significance, low overall risk
of bias, and almost no statistical heterogeneity suggests that there is a correlation between BTX-A and
improvement of pain scores in PDN. Further largescale controlled trials are needed.
Key Words. Neuropathy; Botulinum Toxin; Diabetes; Visual Analogue Scale; Neuropathic Pain

Introduction
Abstract
Objectives. Painful diabetic neuropathy (PDN) is a
debilitating complication of diabetes that greatly
affects the quality of life of those afflicted. There are
many treatment options for neuropathic pain.
Recent studies show a promising analgesic effect
using botulinum toxin-A (BTX-A) for neuropathic
pain.

Approximately 29.1 million people or 9.3% of the United
States population have been diagnosed with diabetes,
and it is estimated that an additional 8 million people
remain undiagnosed [1]. Although precise statistics on
the prevalence of diabetic neuropathy vary, it is estimated that at time of diagnosis, approximately 8% of
those with diabetes type 2 already have neuropathy,
and that at 25 years, the prevalence jumps to 50% [2].
More than 20% of those with diabetes may develop a
painful diabetic neuropathy (PDN) [3,4]

Neuropathic pain is a consequence of disease affecting
the somatosensory system [5]. It is a debilitating process
that affects quality of life [6–11]. The management of
neuropathic pain is troublesome. In a cross-sectional
study that evaluated the burden of PDN, subjects with
moderate and severe painful PDN had suboptimal levels
of overall health, functioning and well being even after
receiving treatments such as antiepileptics (51.4% of
patients in the study), opioids (33.0% of patients in the
study), and NSAIDs (14.3% of patients in the study) [10].
Painful diabetic neuropathy is caused by lengthdependent axonopathy associated with the metabolic,
vascular and hormonal abnormalities of diabetes mellitus
[12]. An increase in glucose may cause metabolic
defects in glycation/advanced glycosylation endproducts, autooxidation, sorbitol, and diacylglycerol that
cause endoneural fibrosis, cytotoxicity, ischemia,
hypoxia, and decreased neurotrophism [13]
Recently, studies have shown promising effects of analgesia for botulinum toxin type A (BTX-A) and human
investigations are gaining ground for this treatment
[14–16]. BTX-A affects the presynaptic vesicles of neurons by inhibiting the release of certain neurotransmitters such as acetylcholine and the nociceptive
neuropeptides, substance P, calcitonin gene-related
peptide, and glutamate [17]. It also inhibits the expression of vanilloid receptor TRPV1 on the surface of
2

peripheral nociceptors that is responsible for inflammatory hyperalgesia [17,18]. This meta-analysis assesses
the association of BTX-A with reduction of diabetic neuropathic pain.
Methods
This review included class I and class II, prospective,
double-blind, placebo controlled trials based on the
criteria published by the American Academy of Neurology classification of evidence for therapeutic trials
(Table 1) [19].
Primary and Secondary Outcomes
All primary outcomes were patient reported. We followed
the pain visual analogue scale (VAS) that was graded
from 0 if there is no pain to 10 which represents severe
pain. For continuous outcomes, we reported the mean
and standard deviation of the change in pain scores from
baseline to each clinical visit of pain assessment. We
included outcomes at the latest time point recorded.
As a secondary outcome measure, we reported dichotomous outcomes for adverse effects using a 2 3 2 table.
The null hypothesis that BTX-A had no effect on neuropathic pain was tested while evaluating results of the
meta-analysis.

                                                       Selection of Studies

                                                       We included studies with class I and class II level of evi-
                                                       dence, giving priority to randomized, blinded, placebo-
                                                       controlled clinical trials that evaluated botulinum toxin
                                                       treatment and its association with the reduction of dia-
                                                       betic neuropathic pain (Table 2). We excluded random-
                                                       ized or nonrandomized open-ended studies with lower
                                                       class III and class IV level of evidences (Table 3).
                                                       Data Extraction and Management

                                                       At least two authors (S.E.L. and D.N.V.) independently
                                                       audited each publication with emphasis on the follow-
                                                       ing: study characteristics (setting, country of origin, and
                                                       language); design (inclusion and exclusion criteria, treat-
                                                       ment characteristics, allocation, dosage and duration of
                                                       treatment effects); participant characteristics: age, sex,
                                                       ethnicity, comorbidity); and neuropathic pain outcome
                                                       (subjective reporting of pain). We assessed article quality
                                                       independently using the Cochrane Risk of Bias assess-
                                                       ment tool [20].

                                                       Statistics

                                                       We followed the Cochrane recommendation by pool-
                                                       ing the data of treatment outcomes from similar
                                                       studies into a single group to make the meta-
                                                       analysis [21].

                                                       The measured outcomes recorded closest to each time
                                                       point of assessment were analyzed. For example, the
                                                       mean and variance of the pain VAS score at week 3 is
                                                       compared to the mean and variance of the pain VAS
                                                       score at week 4 if both studies only had those data
                                                       available.

Selection of Studies
We included studies with class I and class II level of evidence, giving priority to randomized, blinded, placebocontrolled clinical trials that evaluated botulinum toxin
treatment and its association with the reduction of diabetic neuropathic pain (Table 2). We excluded randomized or nonrandomized open-ended studies with lower
class III and class IV level of evidences (Table 3).
Data Extraction and Management
At least two authors (S.E.L. and D.N.V.) independently
audited each publication with emphasis on the following: study characteristics (setting, country of origin, and
language); design (inclusion and exclusion criteria, treatment characteristics, allocation, dosage and duration of
treatment effects); participant characteristics: age, sex,
ethnicity, comorbidity); and neuropathic pain outcome
(subjective reporting of pain). We assessed article quality
independently using the Cochrane Risk of Bias assessment tool [20].
Statistics
We followed the Cochrane recommendation by pooling the data of treatment outcomes from similar
studies into a single group to make the metaanalysis [21].

Results
Our search produced a total of 159 articles (see Figure 1).
Application of inclusion and exclusion criteria resulted in
three randomized placebo-controlled trials for PDN.
Other neuropathic pain entities such as carpal tunnel syndrome, occipital neuralgia, postherpetic neuralgia, complex regional pain syndrome, and trigeminal neuralgia
were excluded from the meta-analysis. After seeking
unpublished data through correspondence with investigators of the included studies, we have excluded one of
three studies because it was a duplicate.

Included Studies
Combining the two qualifying studies, there were a total
of 58 patients receiving a sum of 76 treatments for PDN
randomly allocated to placebo or BTX-A injected on the
dorsum of the foot [25,26]. The female study population
comprised 51.7% of the study population and the average age was 62 years. The trial period of evaluating the
pain scores lasted between 3 and 12 weeks after treatment administration. The trials allowed continued use of
medications such as insulin, anticonvulsants, and antidepressants as long as they were not changed one month
prior to the beginning of the trial. Botulinum injection
areas were identical in each trial with a fixed injection plan
using a 3 3 4 grid that were equally spaced to demarcate
the injection sites on the dorsum of each foot. Both
included studies used BTX-A for the injections, the Yuan
et al. study used OnabotulinumA while the Ghasemi et al.
study used AbobotulinumtoxinA [25,26].

Risk of Bias
There were unclear risks of bias in both the Yuan et al.
and Ghasemi et al. studies with regards to random
sequence generation and allocation biases [25,26]. Both
of these studies did not point out these procedures for
proper evaluation but claimed that they had been
randomized. To keep the study blind, the physician that
administered the treatments did not participate in the
interview and analysis of the outcomes. Also, the preparation of placebo and botulinum toxin was likewise done
by an independent physician that was not involved with
the review of the patients. All of the included studies
reported patient drop outs including the reasons and
data gathered from them. The included studies provided
all data gathered either in the published paper or
unpublished data sought after contacting the authors.
The included studies passed the test of statistical heterogeneity (Chi2 5 0.12, df 5 1, P 5 0.73; I2 5 0%). The
overall risk of bias is low.
Primary and secondary outcomes
The randomized placebo controlled trials of BTX-A treatment compared to placebo in treatment of PDN showed
a significant overall pain reduction at 3–4 weeks by 1.96
points on the 10-point VAS of pain (295% CI: 23.09 to
20.84) (Figure 2). With a Z 5 3.43 (P < 0.001) we can
reject the null hypothesis and conclude that BTX-A has
a significant effect on PDN. There was only one adverse
event of infection in the injection site but this was not
statistically significant (P 5 0.49) (Figure 3). Small study
effects showed that the studies have no heterogeneity
Chi2 5 0.12, df 5 1 (P 5 0.73), I2 5 0% (Figures 2 and 4).
The risk of randomization bias and allocation bias was
unclear for both studies because both studies failed to
indicate the procedures of the randomization and allocation concealment.
Discussion
Bird et al., showed that “patients with greater pain
require greater changes in VAS”[19]. An improvement of
21.3 VAS points is necessary for a clinically significant
minimum change in pain [28,29] For a concept of
“much better pain”, Salaffi et al. and Jensen et al., demonstrated that a change in VAS scores of 22.0 or an
overall improvement of at least 233% in VAS scores are
clinically significant [30,31].
Our analysis suggests that BTX-A is associated with the
improvement of PDN particularly in the dorsum of the
feet. This clinical benefit shows clinical significance for
“minimum change in pain” with improvement of 21.96
VAS points.
There were only three studies examining the impact of
BTX on PDN, and they have quite small sample sizes.

Ultimately only two studies met criteria for inclusion. Our
results could have biases, particularly given that the metaanalysis is based on assumptions of normality for tests of
significance. Also, the outcomes available were only
aggregate data which is also a source of potential bias.
In conclusion, tests for significance, low overall risk of
bias, and almost no statistical heterogeneity suggests
that there is a correlation between BTX-A and improvement of pain scores in PDN. The selection of class I
studies, while limiting the number of included papers,
gives strength to the evidence, although the improvement in pain scores was small.
Given the small effect size, BTX-A may serve best as an
adjunctive treatment for PDN to first-line modalities (see
Dworkin et al. for a review) [32]. More class I research
with large sample sizes are needed before this therapy
can be universally accepted.
