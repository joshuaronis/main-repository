# Bupivacaine for intradermal wheals in small fiber neuropathy
## The recommendation
**Third choice, behind ropivacaine and behind carrying on with what is already in the syringe — not second.** The reputation that puts bupivacaine second rests on nerve-block durations of eight to twelve hours. In an intradermal wheal of the size actually injected, the measured figure is **125 minutes** — and **139** in the one other laboratory that has measured the same milligram dose — and the arithmetic below brings it lower still. A **wheal** is a small blister of anesthetic raised inside the skin itself rather than in the fat beneath it, so the drug sits among the bare nerve endings that carry the pain; **intradermal** means placed within the skin. **Small fiber neuropathy** is damage to those endings, the thin fibers carrying pain and temperature, which in the length-dependent form begins at the toes and works upward.
- **The volume, not the drug, is what makes bupivacaine long.** **0.2 mL** of **0.25 percent** bupivacaine raised as an intradermal wheal on the volar forearm lasted **125 ± 70 minutes** in 20 volunteers. **3 mL** injected under the skin of the forearm lasted **more than 8 hours**. That is **fifteen times** the volume for about **four times** the duration (see \"Why the eight-hour figure does not apply to a wheal\").
- **Mixing an ester into it costs three-quarters of the duration.** Bupivacaine **0.5 mg** intradermally lasted **139 ± 78 minutes** alone and **36 ± 16** mixed with chloroprocaine **3 mg** — a **74 percent** loss, leaving the mixture no better than the chloroprocaine by itself. **Procaine is an ester too** (see \"Why the eight-hour figure does not apply to a wheal\").
- **At a 0.05 mL wheal the expected figure is around one hour.** Those two measurements together put duration at roughly the square root of volume. A 0.05 mL wheal is a quarter of Sweet's 0.2 mL, and the square root of four is two, so **125 ÷ 2 ≈ 60 minutes** — at or just below the 80-minute-to-2-hour range plain lidocaine already gives on your own measurement (see \"What a wheal of your own size would give\").
- **Ropivacaine beat it intradermally at every concentration tested, in the same ten volunteers.** That is the only head-to-head of the two in skin, and it went one way (see \"What a wheal of your own size would give\").
- **The price argument for it over ropivacaine is much weaker than it looks.** Compared bottle to bottle, ropivacaine costs seven to eight times more. Compared per millilitre of finished **0.25 percent** solution — which is how it is actually used — ropivacaine is about **1.7 times** the price (see \"What to buy in Mexico, and how to make the solution\").
- **It is the harshest of the group in the dish and among the gentlest in people, and both halves of that are softer than they sound.** It sits alone in the highest toxicity band of six agents on a human nerve-derived cell line, and its incidence of transient neurologic symptoms after spinal anesthesia is **1.1 percent** against **16.9 percent** for lidocaine. The 1.1 percent is a crude count among bupivacaine recipients, not a fraction of the review's 2,813 patients (see \"The toxicity paradox\").
- **\"Its sting cannot be buffered\" is wrong as written.** It cannot be brought to body pH — it precipitates above **0.2 mL of 8.4 percent bicarbonate per 10 mL** — but a partial buffer at **1:200** cut injection pain from **30 mm to 22 mm** and from **78 mm to 65 mm** on a 100 mm scale, and **warming the syringe to 37 °C beat buffering outright**, **51 mm against 63** (see \"The sting, and the two things that reduce it\").
- **\"The most painful in the group\" is not what the study behind it shows.** In the pH-matched intradermal comparison usually cited, **lidocaine hurt least and etidocaine hurt most** — putting bupivacaine in the middle of the three tested (see \"The sting, and the two things that reduce it\").
- **The safety ceiling roughly halves, and the warning phase before trouble is shorter.** **175 mg** as a single plain dose, against 300 mg for lidocaine and a 350-to-600 mg label range for procaine — and the label adds three more limits the single-dose figure hides: **225 mg with epinephrine 1:200,000**, **400 mg in any 24 hours**, and no repeat more often than **once every three hours**. At 0.5 percent, 175 mg is **35 mL**; at 0.25 percent, **70 mL** (see \"The dose ceiling that changes when the drug changes\").
- **Levobupivacaine does not rescue it, and cannot be bought here anyway** (see \"Levobupivacaine, and why it settles itself\").
**Three claims in wide circulation about bupivacaine do not survive being checked at source**: that it is the second choice for a long block, that it is the most painful of this group to inject, and that its sting cannot be buffered away. Each is taken to its source below. Correcting the second and third does not move the drug back above lidocaine, because the duration is not there.
**If it is used anyway** — because ropivacaine cannot be had and a longer plain block is wanted — the version to use is **0.25 percent**, made by diluting the 0.5 percent bottle one part to one part sterile saline, in the largest wheal the technique tolerates rather than the highest concentration. This is a prescriber's decision to sign off on; bupivacaine is prescription-only in Mexico and its characteristic toxicity is cardiac.

## Why the eight-hour figure does not apply to a wheal
**The measurement in the right tissue, at a wheal volume.**
Sweet, Magee and Holland (1982) ran a double-blind trial in **20 healthy young volunteers**. Their method sentence is the one that matters: \"Intradermal wheals were raised on the volar aspect of the forearm with 0.2 ml.\" Analgesia to pinprick at the injection site was tested every five minutes until full sensation returned.

None of the differences was significant. Two things follow. The first is the headline number. The second is a useful separate fact: **mixing lidocaine into bupivacaine does not shorten it** — which is not a general rule about mixing, because mixing an ester into it destroys most of it. Nor is it a rule for every pair of amides: in a nerve block, ropivacaine mixed with lidocaine and epinephrine lasted 177 minutes less than the same dose of ropivacaine alone (Yousef et al., 2025, PMID 40859881).
**The ester result, and it is the one that bears on his own syringe.** Kim, Goto and Arakawa (1979) gave 20 volunteers three intradermal solutions in a double-blind trial: bupivacaine **0.5 mg** alone, chloroprocaine **3 mg** alone, and the two mixed. Bupivacaine alone lasted **139 ± 78 minutes**. Chloroprocaine alone lasted **24 ± 11**. The mixture lasted **36 ± 16** — significantly shorter than the same amount of bupivacaine alone, and by the authors' own description similar to the chloroprocaine by itself.
**Work the loss out.** 139 minus 36 is **103**, and 103 ÷ 139 is **74 percent** of the bupivacaine's duration gone. The mixture behaved as though the bupivacaine were not in it. The authors' proposed explanation is competition for non-specific binding sites in and around the nerve — a mechanism, not a measurement.

**Why this matters in his syringe.** **Chloroprocaine and procaine are both esters** — local anesthetics built around an ester link and broken down in plasma — while lidocaine, bupivacaine and ropivacaine are **amides**, broken down in the liver. This is the only intradermal measurement of an ester mixed with an amide, and it went one way and went a long way. **It is not a measurement of procaine with lidocaine**, and it is the closest thing on the record to one: if an ester and an amide go into the same syringe, expect the short drug's duration rather than the long one's. **Injecting them at separate sites costs nothing and avoids the question entirely.**
The standard deviation deserves its own sentence. **±70 minutes on a 125-minute mean** is more than half the mean, so individual volunteers ran roughly 55 to 195 minutes on the same solution in the same skin. Any single result is compatible with a very wide range.
**The measurement at fifteen times the volume.**
Arancibia and colleagues (2024) studied two sequential series of ten volunteers in Chile, injecting **3 mL** into each forearm as what they themselves call a \"wheal\"-type subcutaneous infiltration, and recording puncture pain, injection pain, latency to pinprick and cold, and duration of sensory block. Their result: **\"In all study groups, the skin block lasted more than 8 hours.\"** That covers bupivacaine 0.25 percent, bupivacaine 0.5 percent and levobupivacaine 0.5 percent.
**Work the arithmetic out.** The volume ratio is **3 ÷ 0.2 = 15**. The duration ratio is at least **480 ÷ 125 = 3.8**. Fifteen times the solution per site, for under four times the numbness. To get the eight hours out of bupivacaine, each injection has to become fifteen times larger — which stops being a wheal, moves the drug from dermis into fat, and multiplies the milligrams per session by the same factor.
**A third measurement, and it is shorter again.** Grant and colleagues (2004) injected **0.5 mL of 0.5 percent** standard bupivacaine intradermally in the lower back of six subjects, as the comparator arm of a liposomal formulation trial, and measured a **median duration of 1 hour**. That is half of Sweet's figure at twice the concentration. The two studies disagree by a factor of two on the same drug, and that disagreement is the honest size of the uncertainty.
**And with epinephrine in skin it is about seven hours.** Collins, Song and Mahabir (2013) gave 25 subjects four intradermal solutions on the volar forearm, all containing epinephrine. Onset ran 12 to 29 seconds with no significant difference between them, and **mean duration ran 6 h 38 min to 7 h 25 min**, a difference significant at p=0.036 but clinically narrow. Set against a self-measured 90 minutes to 2 hours from lidocaine with epinephrine in neuropathic leg skin, those two facts are both real and are not reconcilable by pharmacology. The difference is the tissue, and it is the reason every figure on this page is a starting estimate rather than a prediction.

## What a wheal of your own size would give
The two measurements above are the only two points available, and they can be joined.
**Fit a curve through them.** Volume rises by a factor of **15**; duration by a factor of at least **3.8**. If duration rises as some power of volume, that power is **log 3.8 ÷ log 15 = 1.34 ÷ 2.71 = 0.49** — near enough to one half. **Duration rises roughly as the square root of volume.** Halve the volume and you keep about seventy percent of the time; quarter it and you keep about half.
**Now run it downward.** A **0.05 mL** wheal — the size the comparison page describes, twenty of them to a session — is a **quarter** of Sweet's 0.2 mL. The square root of four is two. So **125 ÷ 2 ≈ 60 minutes**.
**What that estimate is and is not.** It is an interpolation between two points from two different studies, in two different tissue layers, with two different definitions of the endpoint, in two countries, forty-two years apart. It is not a measurement. But it is the arithmetic the eight-hour reputation invites, and it runs the wrong way for bupivacaine: at the wheal size in use, the drug's expected block is **at or below the 80-minute-to-2-hour range plain lidocaine already delivers**, on a single-dose ceiling of 175 mg against lidocaine's 300, with a slower onset and a worse sting.
**The one direct comparison against ropivacaine went the same way.** Cederholm, Åkerman and Evers (1994) injected intradermal wheals of plain ropivacaine at 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 percent and plain bupivacaine at 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75 percent into the same ten volunteers, randomised and double-blind, with a saline control and at least three weeks between series. **Plain ropivacaine produced significantly longer dermal analgesia than plain bupivacaine at every concentration tested.** Medians of complete analgesia were 30, 60, 120 and 60 minutes for ropivacaine at 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 percent, against 10 minutes for bupivacaine at every strength ([Ropivacaine Wheals](https://app.notion.com/p/2c010b7903aa83a0b9e3813036c9d26e), 5.1) — complete analgesia rather than full recovery, so shorter than the times to full sensation elsewhere on this page. The vascular reading pointed the same way: blanching was significantly more frequent with plain ropivacaine at all four concentrations, and redness significantly more frequent with plain bupivacaine in a dose-dependent way. Redness is dilated vessels, dilated vessels carry the drug away faster, and that is probably part of the mechanism behind the duration result — though vessel narrowing is not thought to be the main reason ropivacaine lasts: it narrows human arteries no more than lidocaine does, and its protein binding is the likelier explanation ([Ropivacaine Wheals](https://app.notion.com/p/2c010b7903aa83a0b9e3813036c9d26e), 5.8.3).
**So the case for bupivacaine as second choice rests on the one comparison it wins and ignores the one it loses.** It wins against lidocaine and etidocaine, plain, pH-matched, intradermally (Howe and Williams, 1994). It loses to ropivacaine, plain, intradermally, in the same volunteers (Cederholm, 1994). Both are real. Only the second one bears on the question of what to reach for when ropivacaine is on the shelf.

## Does raising the strength help?
**Not between 0.125 and 0.25 percent. Possibly at 0.5. Not at 0.75.** This needs stating carefully, because the claim in circulation — that raising bupivacaine's concentration in skin buys nothing — is not quite what the source says.
**Aps and Reynolds (1976)** gave bupivacaine at **0.125, 0.25 and 0.5 percent** and lignocaine at 0.5, 1 and 2 percent intradermally to **31 volunteers**, double-blind. Two findings:
1. **\"Vasoconstriction was observed more frequently at low concentrations of each drug, and vasodilatation at high concentrations\"** (p\\<0.001). This is the self-cancelling mechanism: the extra drug brings extra vessel widening, and the widening carries the extra drug away.
2. **\"Duration of action was unaffected by concentration, except in the case of bupivacaine 0.5 percent, the effect of which was longer lasting than that of other solutions.\"**
The exception is the part that gets dropped in retelling. On the paper's own abstract, **0.5 percent bupivacaine did outlast the rest**. Against that, the same two authors, writing two years later, describe their own 1976 result as showing that a duration-rises-with-concentration relationship \"has not been observed at higher concentrations of racemic bupivacaine and other local anaesthetics.\" The full text could not be read through any route available here, so the two statements cannot be reconciled from the source. The honest reading is that the concentration–duration curve in skin is flat over most of the range with a possible step at 0.5 percent, and that the size of that step is unknown.
**Three reasons to use 0.25 percent anyway.**
**It is the concentration that was measured at a wheal volume.** Sweet's 125 minutes is a 0.25 percent number. The 0.5 percent numbers come from a 0.5 mL lower-back injection (1 hour) and a 3 mL subcutaneous one (over 8 hours), neither of which is a wheal.
**It halves the milligrams.** Every milligram counts twice here: once against the 175 mg ceiling, and once against the finding that runs through every toxicity study on this page — damage rises with concentration, in every drug, by every method of measurement.
**Above 0.5 percent the vasodilatation gets worse and nothing else improves.** Cederholm found redness with plain bupivacaine rising dose-dependently. Aps and Reynolds attributed the vasodilatation they saw at high concentrations to **irritation**, noting it came with more bleeding and bruising at the injection sites. Buying the 0.75 percent bottle costs more and, in skin, delivers a more irritant solution for no measured gain.

## The toxicity paradox
Bupivacaine is the harshest local anesthetic in the dish and among the gentlest in people. Both halves are true, both are measured, and the reason they do not contradict each other is worth having.
**In the dish it sits alone at the top.** Malet and colleagues (2015) exposed undifferentiated **SH-SY5Y** cells — a human neuroblastoma line used as a stand-in for nerve cells — to six local anesthetics for 20 minutes and read off the concentration that killed half of them. Their conclusion sorts the six into three bands: **least toxic, ropivacaine and articaine; medium, mepivacaine, prilocaine and lidocaine; most toxic, bupivacaine alone.**

**Read the 1.1 percent correctly, because the way it is usually printed is wrong.** The paper's own words are that these figures come from \"summarizing all patients in these trials without further adjustment.\" So **1.1 percent is the share of the bupivacaine recipients who had symptoms — it is not 1.1 percent of 2,813, and it is not the meta-analytic result.** The meta-analytic result is a separate number: **relative risk 6.7 (95 percent CI 2.5 to 18)** for lidocaine against bupivacaine, and 5.5 (2 to 15) for lidocaine against prilocaine. Printing 1.1 percent beside 2,813 invites the reader to multiply them, and that product means nothing.
Two further things in that paper are load-bearing and usually left out. **There were too few trials of tetracaine, procaine and ropivacaine to draw any conclusion** — so ropivacaine's good standing in the dish — which is Malet's; four studies in human cell lines place it above lidocaine ([Ropivacaine Wheals](https://app.notion.com/p/2c010b7903aa83a0b9e3813036c9d26e), 6.1) — has no matching human number here. And **baricity, concentration of the anesthetic, and added vasoconstrictor had no significant influence** on whether symptoms occurred.
**Why the two rankings disagree, in the authors' own words.** Pérez-Castro and colleagues (2009) ran the same kind of experiment and stated the paradox directly: \"Lidocaine, linked to the highest incidence of transient neurological symptoms, was **not** the most toxic LA, whereas bupivacaine, a drug causing a very low incidence of transient neurological symptoms, **was** the most toxic LA in our cell model. This suggests that cytotoxicity-induced nerve injury might have different mechanisms for different LAs and different target(s) other than neurons.\"
That is the resolution. A dish measures what a molecule does to one cell type in twenty minutes at a concentration poured directly onto it. A spinal anesthetic measures what a dose does to a whole person over a day, in a tissue — bare nerve roots in cerebrospinal fluid — that is nothing like dermis. **Neither of them is skin, and nobody has measured damage in skin from an injected anesthetic**; the one skin measurement is of lidocaine patches, which lowered epidermal nerve fiber density in healthy volunteers after 42 days (Wehrfritz et al., 2011, PMID 21530339). The dish ranking is a reason to prefer a gentler drug when one is available; it is not evidence of harm from a wheal.

## The 0.003 percent Schwann cell finding, and what it is worth
**Schwann cells** are the cells that wrap peripheral axons in myelin and support the unmyelinated fibers — which are exactly the fibers lost in small fiber neuropathy. Yang and colleagues (2011) cultured them from neonatal rat sciatic nerve and exposed them to five local anesthetics at **10, 100, 500 and 1000 µM**, reading toxicity at **4, 24, 48 and 72 hours**.
- At **10 µM (0.0003 percent)**, nothing killed Schwann cells, at any exposure time.
- At **100 µM (0.003 percent)**, nothing killed at 24 hours. **Only bupivacaine killed at 48 hours.** At **72 hours, bupivacaine and ropivacaine both did** — ropivacaine at p=0.048.
- At **500 µM (0.015 percent)** and **1000 µM (0.03 percent)**, only bupivacaine and ropivacaine killed at 24 hours; by 48 to 72 hours **all five drugs did**.
**The \"bupivacaine alone\" version of this finding comes from the paper's abstract and does not survive its own results section.** At the longest exposure tested, ropivacaine joined it. That matters here, because the claim in circulation — that bupivacaine is the only agent to kill sheath-forming cells at 0.003 percent — is the version that reads worst for bupivacaine and it is the version the paper's own data qualify.
**What 0.003 percent is, in the terms that matter.** It is **1/83rd** of 0.25 percent and **1/167th** of 0.5 percent. So the concentration at which bupivacaine was toxic sits far below anything that goes into a syringe — which sounds reassuring until the other axis is read.
**The other axis is time, and it is the axis that decides.** The toxicity at 0.003 percent needed **48 to 72 hours of continuous exposure**. A 0.05 mL wheal is cleared from skin in hours. What the finding establishes is that **bupivacaine has the lowest concentration threshold of the group for a time-dependent injury** — not that a wheal delivers that injury.
**The authors themselves disown the ranking.** Their discussion notes that clinical practice uses bupivacaine and ropivacaine at 0.5 to 0.75 percent where it uses lidocaine at 1.5 to 2 percent and chloroprocaine at 2 to 3 percent, and that when the drugs are compared at those equipotent ratios the cell death is similar. Their sentence: \"This suggests that bupivacaine and ropivacaine are not necessarily more neurotoxic than other LAs at clinically equipotent concentrations. It is not clear from our data if one LA is safer than another.\"
**The animal arm is weaker than it is usually reported.** Rats received 0.5 percent (n=4) or 0.75 percent (n=4) bupivacaine, or saline (n=8), through a catheter beside the sciatic nerve at 100 µL an hour for 72 hours. Nerves exposed to bupivacaine showed infiltration by macrophages, and that difference reached significance (p=0.039). **The myelin damage did not**: the chi-square comparing presence or absence of myelin injury between groups was not significant. No axonal damage was seen at all. So the finding is inflammation after three days of continuous infusion, not demonstrated demyelination, and the delivery method is a pump rather than an injection.

## The sting, and the two things that reduce it
**Where bupivacaine actually sits on injection pain.** Howe and Williams (1994) ran a randomised, placebo-controlled, double-blinded comparison of **intradermal 2 percent lidocaine, 0.5 percent bupivacaine and 1 percent etidocaine** in 20 subjects, each with and without epinephrine 1:200,000, with **every solution adjusted to pH 5.95 ± 0.15** so that acidity could not do the explaining. Their results, in their own words: **\"Lidocaine hurt least and etidocaine hurt most. Adrenalized solutions were more painful than plain. For plain solutions bupivacaine lasted longest and lidocaine lasted shortest by far. For adrenalized solutions bupivacaine lasted 27 percent longer than lidocaine and 45 percent longer than etidocaine.\"**
**So among the three agents that trial tested, bupivacaine is in the middle on pain.** It is not the most painful, and the study cannot say whether it is the most painful of the seven agents on the comparison page, because it did not test the other four. The millimetre values are behind a paywall and could not be read.
**The 53 mm figure is real but belongs to a different question.** It comes from Vinycomb and Sahhar's systematic review of **digital nerve blocks** — six studies, 335 blocks — where **bupivacaine with epinephrine had the highest mean injection pain at 53 mm** and lidocaine with epinephrine the lowest at 26 mm. A digital nerve block puts a larger volume beside a nerve at the base of a finger. It is not an intradermal wheal, and it is not plain bupivacaine. The same review gives bupivacaine the **slowest onset of the group at 7.6 minutes**, against 3.1 for lidocaine, which is the one thing in it that transfers cleanly.
**It cannot be brought to body pH.** Peterfreund, Datta and Ostheimer (1989) tested this on the bench: 2-chloroprocaine and lidocaine \"readily alkalinized to near physiological pH without precipitation,\" mepivacaine showed delayed precipitation with an 18-to-20-minute latency at 1.5 percent, and **\"bupivacaine and etidocaine solutions precipitated after the addition of small amounts of sodium bicarbonate and could not be alkalinized to physiologic pH.\"**

**Work the margin out.** Cheney's 0.05 mL per 10 mL is **a quarter** of the 0.2 mL per 10 mL at which AlShammari saw precipitate in every sample. ## Levobupivacaine, and why it settles itself
**Levobupivacaine** is the **S(−) enantiomer** of bupivacaine — one of the two mirror-image forms of the same molecule, isolated and sold on its own. Three facts about it are established, and the third ends the discussion.
**It is genuinely less cardiotoxic.** A 2020 review of its clinical pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics concludes that in vitro, animal and human studies all show lower cardiac and central nervous system toxicity than the racemic mixture, while clinically meaningful differences in potency are minor and most trials show similar duration and quality of block. A randomised double-blind trial of 66 men given **0.25 percent at 50 mL** as infiltration anesthesia found no difference in intraoperative satisfaction, 48-hour pain scores or analgesic use between the two.
**In skin it is either the same or worse.** Arancibia's second series compared **bupivacaine 0.5 percent against levobupivacaine 0.5 percent** at 3 mL: no difference in latency, block over 8 hours in both, and **statistically significantly more pain on infiltration with levobupivacaine**. The mechanistic case that ought to favour it does not survive being pointed at the right comparison. Aps and Reynolds (1978) gave **L(−)- and D(+)-bupivacaine** intradermally to **17 volunteers** across eight concentrations from 0.06 to 7.69 mmol/L (0.002 to 0.25 percent) and found that **L(−) lasted longer than D(+) from 0.48 to 3.84 mmol/L**, and that **only L(−) showed a vasoconstrictor effect**, maximal at 0.48 mmol/L with **pallor at 92 percent of sites**. So the good half of the racemate is the vasoconstricting half — but the comparison that decides a purchase is levobupivacaine against the racemate, not against its own mirror image, and there the durations are the same. A laser Doppler study in ten volunteers, injecting 0.1 mL of each drug at four concentrations, found both biphasic — dose-dependent hyperaemia falling below baseline after 40 minutes — with the only difference at the top end, where **0.75 percent racemic bupivacaine stayed significantly more vasodilated than 0.75 percent levobupivacaine**. That study measured blood flow only. No duration of anesthesia was recorded in it at all.
**It is not marketed in Mexico.** Every product record found is Spanish or Chilean: **Chirocane** (Abbott, Spain), **Levobupivacaína Normon** at 2.5, 5 and 7.5 mg/mL (Spain), **Chirocaína** (Chile). No Mexican brand, no registro sanitario, no pharmacy listing. It belongs on the comparison page as an eighth drug with a \"not sold here\" annotation and nowhere else.

**Two of those four ceilings are label figures and two are not, and the difference matters.** Bupivacaine's **175 mg** and lidocaine's **300 mg** are on their labels. Procaine's is a **range** — the Novocain label gives \"350 mg to 600 mg\" as a single safe total dose for infiltration and carries no separate figure for epinephrine — so 120 mg is anywhere from **20 to 34 percent** of it depending which end you plan against, and the bottom is the end to plan against. Ropivacaine's is **200 mg**: that is what the Naropin label gives for local infiltration and field block. **The 225 mg figure and the 3 mg/kg figure are not on the US label at all** — they come from an institutional protocol table and a cross-national review of what regulators recommend — and the label states no adult mg/kg figure for ropivacaine by any route.
**175 mg is only the first of four numbers on bupivacaine's own label, and the other three are the ones a repeated regimen runs into.** The Marcaine label states: **\"Most experience to date is with single doses of MARCAINE up to 225 mg with epinephrine 1:200,000 and 175 mg without epinephrine; more or less drug may be used depending on individualization of each case.\"** Immediately after it: **\"These doses may be repeated up to once every three hours.\"** And on the day's total: **\"In clinical studies to date, total daily doses have been up to 400 mg. Until further experience is gained, this dose should not be exceeded in 24 hours.\"** So the four figures are **175 mg** plain in a single dose, **225 mg** with epinephrine 1:200,000 in a single dose, **400 mg** in 24 hours, and a **three-hour** minimum between doses. **The single-dose number never binds at a wheal volume. The three-hour interval and the 400 mg day can** — they are what a plan binds against that treats one leg and then the other on the same day, or repeats a session because the first wore off.
**Work the ceiling into millilitres, because that is what gets drawn up.** At **0.5 percent** (5 mg/mL), 175 mg is **35 mL**. At **0.25 percent** (2.5 mg/mL), it is **70 mL**. At 0.05 mL a wheal, 70 mL is 1,400 wheals, so the ceiling will not bind on wheal count. **It binds on total milligrams, and it binds at a lower number than lidocaine trains you to expect.**
**The margin shrinks by more than the ratio suggests.** Lidocaine toxicity announces itself — ringing in the ears, tingling around the mouth, drowsiness — before anything happens to the heart, and it responds to supportive care. **Bupivacaine's characteristic toxicity is cardiac: ventricular arrhythmia and arrest, and it is notoriously resistant to resuscitation.** The number to keep is **175 mg**. The reason to stay well under it is that the warning phase is shorter, not that the number is soft.

## What is still unknown
**What a bupivacaine wheal does in neuropathic leg skin.** Every duration figure on this page comes from normal forearm, lower back or hand skin in healthy volunteers, most of them young. *What would settle it:* a timed measurement on one leg against the current mixture on the other, in the same session, recording return of pinprick sensation at each site.
**Whether the square-root scaling holds.** The 60-minute estimate for a 0.05 mL wheal is an interpolation between two studies that differ in tissue layer, endpoint and country. *What would settle it:* wheals of 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2 mL of the same solution timed side by side in one person — which is a test that can be run at home in an afternoon and would be worth more than any paper cited here.
**Whether 0.5 percent really outlasts 0.25 percent in skin.** The abstract of the one study that tested three strengths says it does; the same authors' later description of their own work says there is no concentration–duration relationship at higher strengths. The full text was not obtainable. *What would settle it:* reading Aps and Reynolds 1976 in full, or a modern repeat.
**Where bupivacaine actually ranks on injection pain among the seven agents.** The trial usually cited compared it only with lidocaine and etidocaine, and etidocaine hurt more. *What would settle it:* an intradermal injection-pain comparison that also includes ropivacaine, mepivacaine, prilocaine and articaine. None exists.
**The millimetre values in Howe and Williams.** The ordering is verified from the abstract; the numbers are paywalled. *What would settle it:* the full text of *J Dermatol Surg Oncol* 1994;20(7):459–64.
**Bupivacaine's own lethal concentration in the Malet dish study.** The 0.95 mM figure could not be read at source. The four other values in that table were recovered from papers citing it; bupivacaine's was not. *What would settle it:* the full text of *Anesth Analg* 2015;120(3):589–96.
**Whether the Schwann cell threshold means anything at wheal exposure times.** The 0.003 percent result needed 48 to 72 hours of continuous contact. *What would settle it:* a time-course at exposures a wheal actually delivers — hours, not days. It has not been done for any drug.
**Whether repeated bupivacaine over months changes skin that has already lost fibers.** Unstudied, as it is for every drug in this group. The only objective measure is **intraepidermal nerve fiber density**, a count of fibers in a small punch biopsy, and its year-to-year variation within one person is large enough to hide a real change of the size at issue. *What would settle it:* fiber density at a treated and an untreated site in the same person, before starting and again after a year.
**Whether any Mexican importer can source levobupivacaine.** *What would settle it:* a direct search of the COFEPRIS registry, which was not queried here — the conclusion rests on the absence of any product record, brand or pharmacy listing.

### Nerve tissue
**Malet A, Faure M-O, Delétage N, Pereira B, Haas J, Lambert G.** The comparative cytotoxic effects of different local anesthetics on a human neuroblastoma cell line. *Anesthesia and Analgesia* 2015;120(3):589–96. ### Injection pain and buffering
**Howe NR, Williams JM.** Pain of injection and duration of anesthesia for intradermal infiltration of lidocaine, bupivacaine, and etidocaine. *Journal of Dermatologic Surgery and Oncology* 1994;20(7):459–64. Standing: 58 citing publications, 12 classified statements, all mentioning — no supporting and no contrasting statement, and no editorial notices.
**Vinycomb TI, Sahhar LJ.** Comparison of local anesthetics for digital nerve blocks: a systematic review. *Journal of Hand Surgery (American)* 2014;39(4):744–751.e5. PMID 24612831. **Cheney PR, Molzen G, Tandberg D.** The effect of pH buffering on reducing the pain associated with subcutaneous infiltration of bupivicaine. *American Journal of Emergency Medicine* 1991;9(2):147–8. ### The dose ceiling
**Marcaine (bupivacaine hydrochloride) injection** — US prescribing information, FDA label 018692s015. — All four bupivacaine figures used above, quoted from Dosage and Administration: \"Most experience to date is with single doses of MARCAINE up to 225 mg with epinephrine 1:200,000 and 175 mg without epinephrine; more or less drug may be used depending on individualization of each case\"; \"These doses may be repeated up to once every three hours\"; \"In clinical studies to date, total daily doses have been up to 400 mg. Until further experience is gained, this dose should not be exceeded in 24 hours.\" Limits: the label frames all four as the extent of accumulated experience rather than as measured thresholds — \"most experience to date is with\", \"until further experience is gained\" — and every one of them comes from surgical infiltration and nerve block, not from repeated small dermal wheals; the three-hour interval is written for a surgical top-up, and nothing on the label addresses a session repeated days apart.
**Naropin (ropivacaine hydrochloride) injection** — US prescribing information, FDA label 020533s038. — The ropivacaine figure used above: **200 mg** for local infiltration and field block, and \"a cumulative dose of up to 770 mg NAROPIN administered over 24 hours is well tolerated in adults when used for postoperative pain management.\" Limits: **the label states no adult mg/kg figure for any route**, so the \"about 3 mg/kg\" that circulates for ropivacaine is not a label figure; the 225 mg that circulates alongside it comes from an institutional protocol table and from a cross-national review of regulators' recommendations, not from this label; the 770 mg is described as a tolerated cumulative dose in postoperative pain management rather than as a maximum.
**Novocain (procaine hydrochloride) injection** — US prescribing information, Hospira, on DailyMed. — The procaine range used above: for infiltration anaesthesia, \"350 mg to 600 mg is generally considered to be a single safe total dose\", and \"THE USUAL TOTAL DOSE DURING ONE TREATMENT SHOULD NOT EXCEED 1,000 MG\" across every route. Limits: the label states no different maximum when epinephrine is added; the 1,000 mg is a per-treatment total across all routes and is the wrong denominator for an infiltration regimen; the range is printed with no derivation and no study is cited for either end of it.
**Xylocaine (lidocaine hydrochloride) injection** — US prescribing information. — Lidocaine plain: 4.5 mg/kg to a maximum of 300 mg as a single dose in an adult; 7 mg/kg to 500 mg with epinephrine. Limits: 4.5 mg/kg binds below about 66 kg, so the governing figure is the lower of the two.
Limits common to all four: these are single-dose infiltration ceilings for healthy adults, derived from plasma concentration studies at surgical infiltration sites rather than from repeated small dermal wheals; absorption from dermis is slower than from intercostal or epidural space, which makes them conservative for this use. Conservatism is the right direction here, because the cardiac toxicity that follows a bupivacaine overshoot resists resuscitation in a way lidocaine's does not.

### What has no source
**The 40.5 mm injection-pain figure for plain bupivacaine.** It circulates beside the 53 mm figure. The 53 mm is traceable to Vinycomb and Sahhar's digital-nerve-block review, where it belongs to bupivacaine *with* epinephrine. **No source was found for 40.5 mm**, and the study usually credited with it, Howe and Williams, is paywalled and reports its results as an ordering rather than as numbers in its abstract.
**The claim that bupivacaine is the most painful to inject of the seven agents on the comparison page.** No study has compared all seven for injection pain intradermally, and the one that is cited for the claim found etidocaine worse.
**Bupivacaine's own lethal concentration in the Malet dish study.** The 0.95 mM value is reproduced above because it is in wide circulation and because the paper's abstract does place bupivacaine alone in the most-toxic band. It was not readable at source, and unlike the other five values in that table it could not be recovered from a citing paper either.
**Any measurement of any of this in neuropathic leg skin.** Every duration, pain and blood-flow figure on this page comes from healthy volunteers' forearms, lower backs or hands.
**Any trial of any local anesthetic by this route in small fiber neuropathy.** Still none, by any design.
