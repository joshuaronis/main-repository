# Mepivacaine for intradermal wheals: how it works and what is known about it
## The recommendation
**Mepivacaine is not the longer wheal it looks like.** The only study that measured it in human skin, by the test you would use — pinprick over an intradermal bleb — found recovery complete or nearly complete **within 2 hours at every concentration tested**, with time to half-recovery levelling off **between about 40 and 70 minutes** (Fairley and Reynolds, 1981). That is in the range of plain lidocaine, and below the **90 minutes to 2 hours** you measured on lidocaine with adrenaline. The **155-minute** figure mepivacaine is usually credited with is dental, and dental anesthesia numbs a tooth through bone (see \"How long does a mepivacaine block actually last?\").
- **The vascular attraction is real, small, and not the thing that sets duration.** At clinical strength mepivacaine adds no vessel widening beyond what the needle alone causes, while lidocaine and bupivacaine add a great deal. But the isomer that lasts longer is not the isomer that constricts harder, so the duration is not coming from the vessel effect (see \"Does the vessel effect explain how long mepivacaine lasts?\").
- **The plain strength sold in Mexico is the irritant one.** Mexico sells **3% plain** and **2% with epinephrine**; there is no plain 2%. At **2.7%**, Fairley found bleeding into the skin in **16 of 28** injections, against 4 in the other 532. Buying vasoconstrictor-free mepivacaine here means buying the strength that visibly damages skin (see \"What is sold in Mexico\").
- **The laboratory advantage over lidocaine does not survive reading the statistics.** In the human cell-line assay, mepivacaine's **4.84 mM** was **not significantly different** from lidocaine's 3.35; the authors placed both in one group (see \"What does the laboratory say about damage to nerve cells?\").
- **If you try it, dilute it.** Three parts saline to one part 3% gives **0.75%**, near the top of Fairley's useful range and well under the irritant threshold, for a loss of **at most about 10 minutes** (see \"The arithmetic of diluting it\").

This document builds from what a local anesthetic does to a nerve, up to what is known about mepivacaine in particular, and works out at the end why the recommendation above follows. Every term is defined before it is used, so it can be read straight through.
## What does a local anesthetic do to a nerve?
A nerve sends a signal by letting sodium ions rush into itself. The ions come through pores in the nerve’s outer membrane called **sodium channels**, and the inrush of positive charge is the electrical signal that travels along the fiber.
A local anesthetic works by sitting inside those pores and plugging them. Lidocaine and mepivacaine both do this, and so does every other drug of their chemical type — the **amides**, named for the chemical link joining the two halves of the molecule. They differ from each other in how long they stay in the tissue, not in what they do while they are there.
The plugging is not permanent. A molecule sits in a pore for a fraction of a second, comes loose, drifts away, and plugs another. Over an hour that cycle repeats thousands of times for every molecule present. So there is no fixed set of blocked channels — at any single instant some fraction of the channels along a stretch of nerve is plugged and the rest are open, and which particular ones are plugged is different an instant later.
Take a concrete case to carry through what follows: a single **wheal** — a small blister of anesthetic raised inside the skin itself rather than in the fat below it, so the drug sits among the bare nerve endings that carry pain. Call it 0.05 mL of solution, raising a blister four to seven millimetres across. An injection placed in that layer is called **intradermal** — within the skin — as against one placed below it in the fat.
What decides the plugged fraction is the **free concentration**: the amount of anesthetic dissolved loose in the fluid around those nerve endings, attached to nothing. The more of it drifting near a channel, the more often a molecule finds the pore, and so the larger the fraction of channels sitting plugged at any given moment.
A nerve signal gets through a patch of nerve only if enough channels open there to pass current onward. Plug a large enough fraction at every instant and the signal dies at that patch; plug a smaller fraction and the ones still open carry it through. So there is a threshold free concentration: above it the signal stops, below it sensation returns.
## What ends a block?
Sensation returns when the free concentration falls below that threshold — with anesthetic still present in the skin, just not enough of it. The block does not end because molecules let go of channels. It ends because too few molecules are left drifting free.
What lowers the free concentration is blood. Blood arrives in the small vessels of the injected skin carrying essentially no anesthetic, picks up dissolved drug as it passes through, and leaves carrying it away.
Two quantities set how fast that happens. One is how much blood passes through per minute. The other is how much each volume of blood picks up, which is set by the concentration it finds there. Multiply them and you have the removal rate — which means the removal rate is proportional to the amount still dissolved in the tissue. Skin holding a lot of anesthetic loses it quickly; the same skin holding a little loses it slowly, at the same blood flow, because each passing volume of blood picks up less.
That proportionality has a consequence worth working out, because everything later in this document turns on it.
Suppose the wheal starts with 100 units of anesthetic, sensation returns when 10 units are left, and a quarter of whatever is present leaves every fifteen minutes. The amounts at each quarter hour run 100, 75, 56, 42, 32, 24, 18, 13, 10 — eight steps of fifteen minutes, so the block ends at two hours.
Now halve the blood flow and hold it there. Each fifteen minutes now removes half as much, so each step takes thirty minutes instead of fifteen: the same eight steps, and the block ends at four hours. **Halving the blood flow through a block for its whole duration roughly doubles its length.** Nothing about the drug changed; only the rate at which blood carried it off.
## Why does the choice of anesthetic change the blood flow at all?
Because these drugs act on the muscle cells in the walls of small blood vessels, not only on nerves. A drug that relaxes those muscle cells widens the vessel and increases flow; one that contracts them narrows the vessel and reduces flow.
Lidocaine widens them. Guinard and colleagues (1992) gave eight volunteers fourteen injections each of 0.2 mL, at separate sites on the abdomen, and measured blood flow through the **capillaries** of the skin — the smallest vessels, where blood actually exchanges material with the surrounding tissue — for the following hour with a laser aimed at the skin, which reads the movement of blood cells passing underneath. Lidocaine raised flow by between 277 and 511 percent, depending on concentration — the abstract reports increases, so to roughly 3.8 to 6.1 times its starting value ([Ropivacaine Wheals](https://app.notion.com/p/2c010b7903aa83a0b9e3813036c9d26e), 4.2). Bupivacaine, a longer-acting relative, raised it by between 242 and 725 percent.
One feature of that study governs how much weight any of its numbers can carry. The injections were **subcutaneous** — placed in the fat below the skin, not within it — so they are not wheals. And the scatter is very large: the figures carry standard errors of ±141 and ±136 for lidocaine, ±193 and ±198 for bupivacaine. With eight volunteers and spread of that size, the study separates a strong vasodilator from a weak one and little finer than that.
A drug that widens the vessels around itself speeds up its own removal, by the proportionality just worked out. That is a large part of why a plain lidocaine wheal is short, and it is what adding adrenaline to lidocaine is for: adrenaline contracts those same muscle cells and cancels the widening. A drug added for that purpose is called a **vasoconstrictor**, literally something that narrows vessels.
## What does mepivacaine do to skin blood vessels?
Less than lidocaine does, and the size of the difference is the reason to be interested in the drug at all.
In that same study, mepivacaine raised flow by between **124 and 316 percent**. The comparison that matters is not against zero but against the two controls the study also ran: plain saline — sterile salt water at the concentration of body fluid, which has no drug effect — injected the same way raised flow by **285 percent**, and a bare needle stick with no injection at all raised it by **260 percent** — to roughly 3.9 and 3.6 times baseline. So a good deal of what any of these injections does to blood flow is the trauma of the needle rather than the drug.
The needle-alone control is the number that reframes the measurement. A bare stick with nothing injected moved flow 260 percent. Mepivacaine's whole range sits around that figure — its top end, 316, is barely above it, and its bottom end, 124, is well below. Whatever mepivacaine is doing to vessels is small against the injury of being injected at all.
These controls are as noisy as the drugs: saline was 285 ± 237 and needle stick 260 ± 121. Saline's standard error is almost as large as its mean. So the honest reading is a ranking, not a set of values — lidocaine and bupivacaine clearly above the controls, mepivacaine not.
The concentrations tested ran from 0.05 to 2 percent. That reaches the lower of the two strengths mepivacaine is sold at but stops short of the higher one, so nothing here measures 3 percent directly. Read against the two controls, the finding is that **the mepivacaine concentrations in clinical use did not raise blood flow any more than saline did, and the lower ones blunted the rise** (Guinard et al., 1992). Lidocaine and bupivacaine raised it well above saline; mepivacaine did not.
The effect reverses with concentration, and the reversal has been mapped. Fairley and Reynolds (1981) gave 23 volunteers 0.1 mL intradermal injections across a wide range of mepivacaine concentrations, from 0.011 percent up to 2.7 percent, observed the colour of the skin five to ten minutes later, and tested pinprick sensation every five minutes until it returned. **At 0.3 percent and below, mepivacaine narrowed the vessels. Above that concentration it widened them.** The narrowing was strongest at **0.1 percent**, and was still measurably stronger than saline at 0.011 percent.
At the top of the range the injections produced visible bleeding into the skin, and the size of that effect is worth stating: **haemorrhagic change occurred in 16 of 28 injections of 2.7 percent L-mepivacaine and 8 of 28 of the D isomer, against 4 times in the remaining 532 injections**. The authors read this as local irritation, and it is the one damage signal in the whole literature that was produced in skin.

What the two studies together support is the weaker and more useful claim: at clinical strength mepivacaine is roughly neutral, adding no widening of its own on top of what the needle does, while lidocaine and bupivacaine add a great deal.
A third study adds a complication to even that. Fruhstorfer and colleagues (1993) injected intradermal lidocaine and mepivacaine into skin whose blood flow had been deliberately raised beforehand, and measured what each did. Both produced only a mild narrowing of the vessels feeding into the capillary bed. Mepivacaine additionally narrowed the vessels draining out of it, which is what produces the visible pallor around a mepivacaine injection. Their conclusion was that only an added vasoconstricting drug narrows the *feeding* vessels enough to slow the removal of anesthetic from the tissue.
The distinction is worth stating plainly, because pallor is easy to mistake for evidence. Narrowing the vessels that drain blood away from a patch of skin makes it look pale. Narrowing the vessels that feed blood into it is what reduces how much anesthetic gets carried off. Mepivacaine does more of the first than lidocaine does; whether it does enough of the second to matter is not established by these studies.
## Does the vessel effect explain how long mepivacaine lasts?
Probably not, and the study that shows this is the same one that mapped the reversal.
Mepivacaine as sold is a mixture of two forms of the same molecule, mirror images of each other, called the L and D isomers. Two molecules can have identical atoms connected in the identical order and still be mirror images that cannot be superimposed, in the way a left hand cannot be superimposed on a right. Fairley’s study injected each isomer separately as well as the ordinary mixture.
**The L isomer produced significantly longer analgesia than the D isomer at 0.3 percent and above. It did not produce more vessel narrowing** (Fairley et al., 1981). The two properties came apart: the form that lasted longer was not the form that constricted harder.
That is a direct test of the explanation, and it fails. Whatever makes mepivacaine last longer than lidocaine in skin, the evidence does not support it being the vessel effect. The honest position is that the mechanism is unresolved. The most likely alternative — and this is an inference rather than something these studies measured — is that mepivacaine simply leaves the body more slowly, which the next section takes up.
## How fast does mepivacaine leave the body?
More slowly than lidocaine, on two separate measurements.
Brown and colleagues (1975) followed both drugs in newborns after their mothers received an epidural — an injection into the space just outside the covering of the spinal cord — and calculated how long each took to fall to half its concentration in the blood. The figure averaged 3 hours for lidocaine and 9 hours for mepivacaine, with mepivacaine’s long-term rate of disappearance roughly three times slower.
Prieto-Alvarez and colleagues (2002) measured blood concentrations in 42 patients after a tourniquet was released on an arm that had been filled with one drug or the other. Five minutes after release the two were comparable. Over the following hour lidocaine’s concentration fell significantly while mepivacaine’s stayed roughly level.
Both measurements are of drug in the bloodstream rather than in skin, and neither tells you directly how fast a wheal empties. What they establish is that the body clears mepivacaine more slowly than lidocaine — which is a property of the molecule, present wherever it sits.
## How long does a mepivacaine block actually last?
### In skin, which is the tissue that matters here
Two studies have measured this, four years apart, from the same laboratory, and they agree. Fairley and Reynolds is the larger and the one that maps the concentration range; Willatts and Reynolds timed racemic mepivacaine against five other drugs in the same volunteers. **Between them they cover 33 volunteers, and neither found a mepivacaine bleb lasting more than two hours.**
Fairley and Reynolds's endpoint was **time to 50 percent recovery**: two blebs were each pricked five times, and the clock stopped when five of those ten pricks were felt as sharp again. That is a half-recovery time, not the end of the block, so it understates total duration — but they also followed every bleb to full recovery.
**Recovery was complete or almost complete within 2 hours at every concentration tested, 2.7 percent included.** Time to 50 percent recovery rose with concentration and then flattened. The ceiling of that curve was **about 40 minutes for the D isomer and above 70 minutes for the L isomer**, and the racemic mixture that is actually sold — an equal blend of the two — fell between them (Fairley and Reynolds, 1981).
**The second study is shorter still and puts mepivacaine beside its rivals.** Willatts and Reynolds (1985) gave 10 volunteers intradermal 0.1 mL injections of six agents at three concentrations each — racemic mepivacaine, lignocaine and racemic prilocaine at 0.1, 0.33 and 1.0 percent, alongside three others — double-blind, pinprick tested until sensation returned. Their sentence covers every drug and every subject: **\"Complete recovery from all injections had occurred by 80 min.\"** They also fitted duration against the logarithm of concentration, and **mepivacaine's slope is the steepest of the six at 31.12 ± 5.51 extra minutes per tenfold rise**, against lignocaine's 18.47 ± 2.53 (difference between slopes P under 0.001) — which is the vasoconstriction showing up as duration, and is the strongest thing that can be said for the drug in this tissue. **It is also worth very little in practice**: a doubling of concentration is 0.30 of a tenfold step, so it buys **0.30 × 31.1 ≈ 9 minutes**, and mepivacaine still finished inside 80 minutes at 1 percent.
Those are the numbers to hold against the dental figures below, because they were taken in the right tissue, by the right route, with the right test, on 0.1 mL blebs that differ from a 0.05 mL wheal only in size. The dental figures are longer by an hour or more. That gap is the tissue, not the drug.

### In dentistry, where the usual figures come from
In the setting where plain mepivacaine is routinely used — dentistry, where 3 percent plain mepivacaine exists specifically so that a vasoconstrictor can be avoided — it lasts roughly as long as lidocaine with adrenaline.
A small trial compared 3 percent plain mepivacaine against 2 percent lidocaine with adrenaline in twenty patients each, and measured 155 minutes of anesthesia against 138.6, with mepivacaine also taking effect faster, at 2.3 minutes against 2.95 (Pillai et al., 2016). A trial of forty patients compared the two drugs with the *same* adrenaline concentration in both and found onset faster with mepivacaine, at 4.2 minutes against 4.6, and duration not significantly different, at 177 minutes against 167 (Barath et al., 2015).
A **meta-analysis** — a study that pools the results of earlier trials and analyses them together, so that many small trials can answer a question none of them could answer alone — sharpens this. It pooled 28 randomized dental trials (Su et al., 2014). Against 2 percent lidocaine with adrenaline at 1:100,000:
- **3 percent plain mepivacaine** had a *lower* success rate, a shorter onset time, and was more painful during injection.
- **2 percent mepivacaine with the same adrenaline** had a *higher* success rate, a similar onset time, and was *less* painful during injection.
Read those two rows together and the finding is that plain mepivacaine is close to but not quite equal to lidocaine-with-adrenaline, while mepivacaine-with-adrenaline beats it. The interesting figure for anyone wanting to avoid a vasoconstrictor is the first row: plain mepivacaine gets close, at the cost of a somewhat lower chance of a complete block.
None of these measurements is in skin. Dental anesthesia numbs a tooth through bone and mucous membrane, which is a different tissue with a different blood supply. What transfers is the ranking between the drugs rather than the number of minutes.
## The arithmetic of diluting it
Fairley's study gives the one thing needed to work out what a dilution costs, which is the slope of the dose-duration line.
Over the range where the relationship is straight — 0.1 to 0.9 percent — duration rises by a fixed number of minutes for every tenfold rise in concentration. For the racemic mixture sold, that slope is **16.0 minutes per tenfold change**. The two isomers differ: 22.7 minutes for L, 11.9 for D, which is why the blend sits between them.
So the cost of diluting 3 percent down to 0.75 percent, a fourfold cut, is that slope multiplied by the log of four. The log of 4 is 0.60, and 0.60 × 16.0 is **9.6 minutes**.
That is an overestimate, and knowingly so. The slope of 16 minutes holds in the straight middle of the curve; above 0.9 percent the curve has already bent over toward its ceiling, so the stretch from 3 percent down to 0.9 percent costs almost nothing and only the last short step costs anything at all. **Ten minutes is the ceiling on what a fourfold dilution takes away.**
Against that, the dilution moves the injection from 3 percent — above the 2.7 percent at which more than half of Fairley's blebs bled — to 0.75 percent, where none did. The trade is heavily one-sided.
1. Draw 0.45 mL from a 1.8 mL cartridge of 3 percent.
2. Add 1.35 mL of sterile saline, giving 1.8 mL at 0.75 percent.
3. That yields **36 wheals** of 0.05 mL, each containing **0.375 mg** of mepivacaine.
Diluting a dental cartridge means breaching a sealed single-use container, which is the step that introduces contamination risk, and it should be done with a prescriber rather than improvised.
## What does the laboratory say about damage to nerve cells?
Every local anesthetic kills nerve cells at a high enough concentration for a long enough exposure. Laboratory studies compare them by finding the concentration at which each does a given amount of damage: a higher figure means more drug was needed, and therefore a gentler drug.
On this measure mepivacaine does well, and in one study extremely well.
Radwan and colleagues (2002) exposed sensory neurons from chick embryos to four anesthetics and scored the collapse of the growing tip of the nerve fiber. The concentration producing collapse in half the cells was about 10 times higher for mepivacaine than for lidocaine, bupivacaine or ropivacaine — the three of which sat close together. Their stated conclusion was that mepivacaine was safer than all three.
Kasaba and colleagues (2003) exposed cultured neurons from a freshwater snail to seven anesthetics and ranked them by increasing damage: procaine and mepivacaine were least toxic and equal to each other, then ropivacaine and bupivacaine equal to each other, then lidocaine, then tetracaine, then dibucaine.
Malet and colleagues (2015) measured the concentration lethal to half the cells in a human nerve-derived cell line after twenty minutes. In millimoles per litre — a count of molecules rather than milligrams — bupivacaine was 0.95, lidocaine 3.35, prilocaine 4.32, **mepivacaine 4.84**, articaine 8.98 and ropivacaine 13.43.
That list reads as a ranking, and the ranking is misleading. **The authors reported no significant difference between the figures for prilocaine, lidocaine and mepivacaine**, and grouped all three together as the middle of three toxicity bands: ropivacaine and articaine least toxic, prilocaine and lidocaine and mepivacaine in the middle, bupivacaine most toxic. Mepivacaine's 4.84 against lidocaine's 3.35 is a gap the study explicitly declined to call real. What the study does establish is that both are far gentler than bupivacaine and clearly behind ropivacaine.
So of the three laboratory studies, two rank mepivacaine ahead of lidocaine and the third — the only one performed on human cells — finds them indistinguishable. That is a weaker laboratory result than it first appears, and it matters, because the size of the laboratory-versus-human contradiction depends on how large the laboratory advantage was to begin with.
## What does the human evidence say about nerve damage?
The opposite, and this is the finding that changes the conclusion.
The human measurement available is **transient neurologic symptoms**: new back pain radiating into the buttocks or legs, appearing within about a day of a spinal anesthetic that was otherwise uneventful, and lasting one to three days. A spinal anesthetic puts anesthetic directly into the fluid around the nerve roots. Nerve conduction studies in people with these symptoms come back normal, so the symptoms are not proof of damage — but the rate at which a drug produces them is the closest thing to a human ranking of anesthetic irritation that exists.
Individual trials disagree with each other by two orders of magnitude. One randomized trial found symptoms in none of 30 patients given 1.5 percent mepivacaine against 6 of 27 given 2 percent lidocaine (Liguori et al., 1998). A review of 679 mepivacaine spinal anesthetics at one hospital found a single case, 0.14 percent (Sankar et al., 2018). A trial of 4 percent mepivacaine against 0.5 percent bupivacaine found symptoms in 30 percent against 3 percent.
The pooled analyses are what settle the question, and they do not favour mepivacaine.
- A meta-analysis of 29 randomized trials covering 2,813 patients found the rate 16.9 percent after lidocaine and **19.1 percent after mepivacaine**, against 1.1 percent after bupivacaine and 1.7 percent after prilocaine, and concluded that prilocaine and bupivacaine are associated with fewer symptoms than lidocaine *and mepivacaine* (Eberhart et al., 2002). Two things about that 19.1 percent decide how much it can carry. It is a **crude incidence** — every patient who received mepivacaine in any of those trials, added together without adjustment — rather than a pooled comparison, so lidocaine's 16.9 and mepivacaine's 19.1 largely come from *different trials in different populations* and were never randomized against each other. And the authors did compute proper pooled relative risks, but only two of them: lidocaine against bupivacaine at **6.7** and lidocaine against prilocaine at **5.5**. **No pooled relative risk for mepivacaine was reported.** The 19.1 percent is the highest figure in the group and it is also the least adjusted one.
- That same analysis answers the question of whether mepivacaine's signal is really a concentration effect. It is not, on this evidence: **baricity, concentration of the local anesthetic, and the addition of vasoconstrictors all had no significant influence on the rate** (Eberhart et al., 2002). Baricity means whether the solution is heavier or lighter than spinal fluid, which decides where in the spinal canal it pools. The common explanation — that mepivacaine looks bad only because the bad numbers came from concentrated or hyperbaric preparations — is not supported by the analysis that produced the figure.
- A meta-analysis of 39 randomized trials covering 4,733 patients found lidocaine produced symptoms about four times as often as its comparators overall — but in the breakdown by drug, lidocaine was worse than every other anesthetic **except mepivacaine**, ropivacaine and one other (Koo et al., 2020). Mepivacaine was not distinguishable from lidocaine.
- A third pooled analysis compared every anesthetic against every other, including pairs that had never been tested head to head, by chaining the comparisons that had been. Across 24 trials and 2,226 participants it found the risk lower than lidocaine’s for bupivacaine, levobupivacaine, prilocaine, procaine and ropivacaine, with risk ratios from 0.10 to 0.23 (Forget et al., 2019). Mepivacaine is absent from that list, and the reason it is absent is worth stating exactly, because \"absent\" is not the same as \"as bad as lidocaine\". Its risk ratio against lidocaine was **1.01, with a 95 percent confidence interval running from 0.18 to 5.82**, from **4 studies**, graded **very low quality**. A confidence interval is the range the true value is expected to lie in; this one spans a fivefold reduction to a nearly sixfold increase. Mepivacaine is missing from the safer list not because it was shown to be as harmful as lidocaine but because the trials cannot tell the two apart in either direction.
- A meta-analysis comparing mepivacaine directly against bupivacaine screened 406 papers and included 14 randomized trials with 1,007 patients in total, and found mepivacaine associated with about nine times the odds of these symptoms (odds ratio **9.18**, 95 percent confidence interval **2.42 to 34.88**) (Tan et al., 2022). Three qualifications travel with that number. The 1,007 patients are the whole review; the odds ratio comes only from the subset of those trials that reported the outcome, which is smaller. The outcome is a **composite** of transient neurologic symptoms *and* transient nerve root irritation, not the former alone. And the comparator is bupivacaine, the gentlest drug in the group, so the ratio measures the distance from the best case rather than from lidocaine.
- A second meta-analysis of the same comparison, over 7 randomized trials and 672 patients, found a risk ratio of **3.90** with a confidence interval of **0.94 to 16.22**, which does not reach significance (Fu et al., 2022). Two pooled analyses of mepivacaine against bupivacaine, one significant and one not. That is a literature that has not settled.
- The largest head-to-head comparison in one setting found much smaller differences than any of the pooled work. Among 1,217 hip and knee replacements at a single high-volume centre, symptoms appeared after **6.9 percent** of lidocaine spinals, **9.2 percent** of mepivacaine and **4.1 percent** of bupivacaine, a difference that did not reach significance (Slaven et al., 2023). The ordering matches the pooled data — mepivacaine nominally worst — but at rates half those Eberhart reported, and with the three drugs statistically indistinguishable.
So the laboratory ranks mepivacaine at or above lidocaine and the pooled human data ranks it at or below. That is the central contradiction in this drug, and it has to be sized before it can be explained.
**It is smaller than it looks from both ends.** On the laboratory side, the one assay run on human cells found mepivacaine and lidocaine not significantly different, so two of the three studies carry the advantage and the most relevant one does not. On the human side, mepivacaine's worst figure is a crude unadjusted incidence with no pooled comparison behind it, its network estimate against lidocaine is 1.01 with an interval running from 0.18 to 5.82, and the largest single-centre comparison puts the three drugs statistically level. The reversal is not laboratory-gentle against human-harsh. It is laboratory-slightly-gentle against human-cannot-tell.
**Of the explanations available, the route is the one that survives.** Three were candidates.
1. **The dish.** Laboratory studies expose isolated cells directly, which removes everything the body does to distribute, dilute and clear a drug. This holds, but it applies equally to every drug in the comparison, so it explains why laboratory numbers are not clinical numbers without explaining why mepivacaine in particular changes rank.
2. **Concentration.** The attractive explanation is that mepivacaine looks bad only because the damaging trials used strong or hyperbaric preparations — the 30 percent figure came from 4 percent mepivacaine while the lowest came from 1.5 percent. **This one fails at the source.** Eberhart's analysis tested it directly and found concentration, baricity and added vasoconstrictors all without significant influence on the rate. The signal does not track the strength of the solution.
3. **The route.** Spinal anesthesia injects into the fluid around bare nerve roots, which have no protective sheath and sit in a compartment that does not clear drug quickly. A wheal puts a fortieth of a millilitre into skin threaded with capillaries that empty it within the hour. These are not the same exposure, and the drug that behaves worst in a still pool of fluid need not be the drug that behaves worst in well-perfused tissue. **This is the explanation that fits, and it is also the one that makes the whole spinal literature only weakly relevant to a wheal.**
One piece of evidence does bear on damage in skin, and it is not reassuring about the strength sold. Fairley's 2.7 percent injections produced bleeding into the skin in more than half of them, against four times in over five hundred injections at lower strengths, and the authors read this as local irritation. That is a skin measurement, in humans, at essentially the concentration Mexico sells plain. It says nothing about nerve fibre loss, which is the damage that would matter to someone whose fibres are already depleted — **nobody has measured damage to nerve fibres in skin from an injected anesthetic**; the one measurement is of lidocaine patches, which lowered epidermal nerve fibre density in healthy volunteers after 42 days (Wehrfritz et al., 2011, PMID 21530339) — but it does say that 3 percent mepivacaine is hard on the tissue it is put into.
## What does all of this mean for a wheal?
Now the pieces can be put together.
**The reason to want mepivacaine was that it does not open up skin blood vessels the way lidocaine does.** That is real and measured: at clinical strength it adds no vessel widening beyond what the needle itself causes, while lidocaine at its higher concentrations raises flow to roughly 1.8 times what saline alone produced — though at lidocaine’s lowest concentration the two were comparable, so the gap is a property of the strengths actually used rather than of the molecule at every dose. Since removal is proportional to blood flow, and halving the flow through a block roughly doubles its length, a drug that does not raise the flow in the first place should outlast one that does — without any adrenaline being added.
**The clinical numbers are consistent with that.** In one small trial plain mepivacaine *outlasted* lidocaine with adrenaline, 155 minutes against 138.6 — about 12 percent longer, with no vasoconstrictor in it at all. In another, with both drugs carrying the same adrenaline, the two durations were not significantly different. For someone who wants a longer block than plain lidocaine gives without introducing a vasoconstrictor, that is the entire attraction.
**But the mechanism that made the story attractive does not survive testing.** The isomer that lasts longer is not the isomer that constricts harder, so the duration is not coming from the vessel effect. It is more likely coming from slower clearance, which the plasma measurements support but do not prove for skin. This does not make the duration less real; it makes the reasoning behind it weaker than it looked.
**And the duration, measured in skin, is not there.** This is what decides it. The dental figures are the ones that made mepivacaine interesting, and they come from a tooth socket. The single measurement taken in human skin, intradermally, by pinprick, found half-recovery levelling off between about 40 and 70 minutes and full recovery inside 2 hours at every concentration up to 2.7 percent. A wheal of plain 3 percent mepivacaine should be expected to behave like a wheal of plain lidocaine, not like a dental block, and not like the 90 to 120 minutes already obtained from lidocaine with adrenaline.
**The safety argument does not reverse so much as dissolve.** The reason to prefer mepivacaine over lidocaine for repeated injection into skin that has already lost nerve fibers was that laboratory assays rank it gentler. The human cell assay does not, the pooled spinal data cannot resolve the comparison in either direction, and the spinal route is the wrong exposure to reason from anyway. What is left pointing at skin is Fairley's finding that the plain strength on sale bleeds the tissue.

**What follows.** Mepivacaine is not worth switching to for duration, because the only skin measurement puts it at or below what is already being obtained. It is not worth switching to for safety, because the laboratory advantage over lidocaine is not significant in human cells and the human evidence cannot separate them. Its one genuine and well-measured property — that it adds no vasodilation of its own, which is exactly what someone who has rejected epinephrine wants — is real, but it buys a block of ordinary length rather than a long one, and in Mexico it is only sold vasoconstrictor-free at the strength that irritates skin.
If it is tried regardless, it should be tried **diluted to about 0.75 percent**, which costs at most ten minutes and moves the injection off the irritant end of the range, and it should be tried as a **same-day comparison against the current solution**, one leg each, timing the return of pinprick sensation. That single measurement would settle in an afternoon what the entire literature above cannot, because it would be taken in the right tissue on the right person — the same reason the measured 1.3-fold gain on adrenaline outranks the dental studies that promised two to three.

**There is no plain 2 percent.** This is not a gap in Mexican supply but how the drug is sold worldwide: 3 percent is the strength made without a vasoconstrictor and 2 percent is the strength made with one. Anyone who wants mepivacaine without epinephrine is buying 3 percent, which is the strength at which Fairley's injections bled the skin. Getting a lower plain concentration means diluting it (see \"The arithmetic of diluting it\").
**The ceiling on dose.** The Mexican labelling gives a maximum of **7 mg/kg over 1.5 hours**, not exceeding **400 mg** in a single administration, and **1 g in a day** for an adult. At 3 percent, a 0.05 mL wheal holds **1.5 mg**, so the 400 mg limit is reached at **266 wheals** and one cartridge supplies **36**. For a 70 kg adult the weight-based figure would be 490 mg, so the 400 mg absolute cap binds first. The ceiling does not constrain wheal work at any plausible number of injections, but it is the figure to check against if volumes ever rise, and it should be confirmed with a prescriber rather than taken from this page.

## What is not known

**How long a mepivacaine wheal lasts on these legs.** Duration in human skin has been measured once, by Fairley and Reynolds, on forearms of healthy volunteers in their twenties, at half-recovery rather than full block. Neuropathic skin on a leg is not that tissue. What would settle it: a same-day comparison of mepivacaine against the current solution, one on each leg, timing the return of pinprick sensation.
**Nobody has measured skin blood flow after a mepivacaine wheal for longer than an hour.** The Guinard recordings stop at 60 minutes and the Fairley colour observations were made at 5 to 10 minutes. Everything said here about vessels applies to the first hour of a block.
**Nothing measures 3 percent directly for blood flow.** Guinard's concentrations stopped at 2 percent and Fairley's vascular scoring at 2.7 percent. The strength actually sold plain in Mexico sits above both.
**Whether mepivacaine damages nerve fibers in skin is unmeasured.** Fairley's haemorrhagic changes show tissue irritation at 2.7 percent but say nothing about fiber loss, which is the damage that matters to skin already depleted of fibers. What would settle it: skin biopsy fiber counts before and after a course of repeated wheals. No such study exists for any local anesthetic.
**Whether the spinal evidence transfers to skin at all is unknown,** and the argument of this page is that it probably does not. What would settle it: any measurement of anesthetic irritation in skin comparing two drugs. None exists.

### What has no source
**That mepivacaine does not raise blood flow at 3 percent.** Guinard's concentrations stop at 2 percent and Fairley's vascular scoring at 2.7 percent. The strength sold plain in Mexico was measured by neither, and Fairley's data has vasodilation increasing across the top of the range, so the neutrality claim is an extrapolation in the unfavourable direction.
**That a 0.05 mL wheal behaves like Fairley's 0.1 mL bleb.** Halving the volume halves the drug and shortens the diffusion distance to the clearing capillaries. Fairley's durations are therefore an upper bound on what a smaller wheal gives, not an estimate of it.
**That anything measured in spinal anesthesia predicts anything in skin.** This page argues it does not. No study has tested the assumption in either direction.
**That diluting a cartridge is safe to do at home.** The dilution arithmetic is sound; the sterility of the procedure is not addressed by any source here.
**That the 2 percent with epinephrine holds a Mexican registro.** It is on sale in Mexican dental depots but no registro number was located for that presentation, and only the 3 percent appears in Vademecum México.
**That Carbocaína is unavailable in Mexico.** No Mexican registration was found in the searches run, which is weaker than a check of the COFEPRIS register itself.
Several entries above still rest on abstracts and citing papers rather than on the full articles — Pillai, Barath, Brown, Prieto-Alvarez, Radwan, Kasaba, Su, Fruhstorfer and Covino — and Eberhart rests on its English abstract because the paper is in German. Fairley, Guinard, Malet, Forget, Koo, Tan, Fu, Slaven and Singh have been checked against the source record and carry PMIDs and DOIs. Verify the rest before citing them anywhere that matters.
