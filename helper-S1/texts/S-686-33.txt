Common Peroneal Nerve Block for Sciatica

Kazuya Tajiri, MD, PhD*; Keisuke Takahashi, MD, PhD**; Kazuo Ikeda, MD, PhD†; and Katsuro Tomita,
MD, PhD†

The effectiveness of common peroneal nerve block for lumbar disc herniation was evaluated in a
double blind study. Common peroneal nerve block was performed near the fibular head in nine
patients using 2% lidocaine and in 10 patients using saline. The average pain scale score decreased
from 3.1 to 0.6 in the lidocaine group, whereas it decreased from 3.0 to 2.6 in the placebo group.
The average result in the straight leg raising test increased from 61° to 84° in the lidocaine
group, but from 44° to 50° in the placebo group. Lower leg pain lessened more in the lidocaine group
than in the placebo group. The finding that lower leg pain disappeared or decreased with a lidocaine
block at a site far distal to the lumbar lesion suggests that impulses that are transmitted distal
to the lesion may be important for the generation of sciatic radicular pain.

Many kinds of nerve block are used for the treatment of pain. In general, an anesthetic is thought
to be effective for reducing pain when injected proximal to the lesion. However, some nerve blocks,
such as nerve root block, intercostal nerve block, and trigeminal nerve block, are performed distal
to the lesion. It is not clear why these distal blocks are effective, but it is possible that the
local anesthetic reaches the lesion and blocks the nerve. The authors performed a prospective
evaluation of the effectiveness of common peroneal nerve block for patients with lumbar disc
herniation. The current study investigated the effectiveness of nerve block at a point distal to the
lesion. The fibular head was selected as the injection site because it is far from the lumbar nerve
root, and the common peroneal nerve takes a superficial course at that point.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Between October 1991 and March 1992, 22 consecutive outpatients who had diagnoses of sciatic
radicular pain on physical examination were enrolled in this study. All of the patients underwent
myelography or magnetic resonance imaging, and those who did not have intervertebral disc
herniation at L4-L5 or L5-S1 were excluded. The existence of a sensory or motor deficit and the
results of the straight leg raising test were not primary selection criteria. However, if a sensory
or motor deficit existed, the distribution had to be compati-

ble with the dermatome of the affected nerve root detected by imaging. Patients without sensory or
motor deficits had to have pain in the typical dermatomal distribution of the affected nerve root.
The patients were divided randomly into lidocaine and placebo groups after enrollment.

For common peroneal nerve block, 6 ml of 2% lidocaine (which was thought to be enough to block the
nerve completely) or 6 ml of physiologic saline was injected 3 cm distal to the fibular head. To
avoid nerve injury, injection was done near the nerve at a site that did not cause radiating pain.

Changes in sensitivity to pin prick (analgesia, 0 points; normal sensation, 10 points) in the
distribution of the common peroneal nerve and weakness of the tibialis anterior and extensor
hallucis longus muscles were used as indicators of blockade. Patients who showed no changes of
sensation and muscle strength at 15 minutes after nerve block were excluded from this study. The
intensity of pain in the lower leg was determined before and at 15 minutes after nerve block using a
five grade pain scale (Table 1).7 The straight leg raising test also was done. The physician
assessing the effect of the block had no knowledge of the type of therapy given.

Statistical Analysis

The mean pain scale scores obtained before and after nerve block were compared using Student's t
test. The relationship between changes of the pin prick scale and the decrease in pain was examined
using Spearman's correlation analysis. P values less than 0.01 were considered significant.

RESULTS

Twenty-two patients took part in this study. Three patients in the lidocaine group who did not show
a change of muscle strength and sensation at 15 minutes after nerve block were excluded. The average
age of the patients in the lidocaine group (N = 9), was 41.8 years (range, 22-69 years); in the
placebo group (N = 10), the average age was 41.0 years (range, 18-70 years). The L5 nerve root was
involved in seven patients from both groups, whereas the S1 nerve root was involved in two patients
from the lidocaine group and three patients from the placebo group.

DISCUSSION

pain, despite that the block was done at a point distal to the nerve root. A placebo effect has been
reported to occur in one of three cases.3 In this study, lower leg pain decreased in four of 10
patients in the placebo group. In contrast, the common peroneal nerve block was effective in

tients in the lidocaine group. Sciatic radicular pain means pain in the leg arising from a lumbar
nerve root lesion.8 The pathophysiology of the lesion is complex.13 Impulses generated at the lesion
are transmitted in the proximal and distal directions,4,15 but the actual mechanism that generates
radicular pain remains unknown. The effectiveness of distal blocks, such as nerve root block,
intercostal nerve block, and trigeminal nerve block, was not recognized until recently. It is
possible that in some cases the local anesthetic reaches the lesion and blocks the impulses just at
the lesion. In the case of nerve root block, nerve root infiltration often shows that the contrast
medium reaches the lesion. Selander and Sjostrand14 reported that subepineurally injected local
anesthetics spread 6.5 cm after 15 minutes in rabbits.

In this study, the fibular head was selected as the site for the block because it is far from the
nerve root. Thus, it seems reasonable to assume that the local anesthetic does not reach the lesion
after common peroneal nerve block. Local anesthetics block the transmission of nerve impulses by
interfering with membrane depolarization.12 When local anesthetic is injected proximal to a lesion,
the impulses to the central nervous system are blocked and the patient does not feel pain. When the
injection is given distal to the lesion, impulses to the brain obviously are not blocked.

Xavier et al16 reported that sciatic nerve block was effective in five cases of sciatic radicular
pain. Three hypotheses regarding the mechanism involved in the generation of sciatic radicular pain
have been proposed. These are: (1) loss of segmental inhibition in the spinal cord6,10; (2) so
called cross talk9,11; and (3) amplification of normal impulses.1 All of these hypotheses require
impulses from the periphery but do not explain the nature of such impulses.

Lewis5 explained the mechanism of causalgia as follows. Impulses generated at the site where a
peripheral nerve is stimulated chronically are transmitted in the proximal and distal directions. In
the case of sensory nerves, after impulses are transmitted to the periphery, a vasodilator substance
(H substance) is released from cells in the skin. This substance decreases the pain threshold, and
generates impulses from sensory nerve ends, so causalgia occurs.

Ectopic impulses generated by affected nerve roots are transmitted to the periphery.4 Recently,
Devor2 reported that axonal swellings acted as ectopic neural pacemaker nodules in chronically
injured rat sciatic nerves. It seems possible that new impulses are generated in afferent sensory
nerve fibers after antidromic impulses reach the distal nerve ends. These impulses are transmitted
to the brain and cause the sensation of sciatic radicular pain (Fig 3).

The mechanism of the generation of sciatic radicular pain remains unknown. However, the finding that
nerve block at a site distal to the lesion is effective for pain relief shows that impulses that are
transmitted to the periphery may be important in the generation of sciatic radicular pain.
