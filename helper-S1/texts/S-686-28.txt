Abstract.
OBJECTIVE: Supplementing a local anaesthetic with epinephrine has advantages in hand surgery: faster onset of anaesthesia,
extended effect of anaesthetics with prolonged pain reduction, decreased bleeding into the operative field, and abandoned need
for tourniquet. We hypothesized that the use of ropivacaine with epinephrine additive in digits allows good skin perfusion rates
with prolonged pain reduction.
DESIGN: In this prospective, double-blinded, randomized study with 20 volunteers (80 fingers, without operation) the chrono-
logical course of changes in digit blood flow, post injection pain status and complication rates after two-injection dorsal technique
anaesthetic block with ropivacaine (group 1: ropivacaine 0.75%, group 2: ropivacaine 0.75% and epinephrine 1 : 1.000.000, group
3: local tumescent anaesthesia 0.15% TLA: ropivacaine, lidocaine, saline solution plus epinephrine 1 : 1.000.000) were examined.
As a control group, injection of 2% lidocaine in the way with 20 minutes tourniquet (group 4) was used.
MAIN OUTCOME: Group 1 showed increased skin perfusion with a maximum of 160.2%, group 2 showed no significant
changes with a following significant increase of skin perfusion (+66.6%). In group 3, a significant reduction (–54.1%) was
followed by an increase of skin perfusion (+45.7%). In the control group (group 4) the skin perfusion was decreased (–66.2%)
and increased significantly after opening the tourniquet (+248.1%). The longest pain reduction was shown for ropivacaine 0.75%
and epinephrine with 18.0 hours (group 2); the other groups were between 4.6 to 8.1 hours. Conclusion: Using ropivacaine with
epinephrine additive in digits could improve the intra-, and postoperative terms.
Keywords: Digital nerve block anaesthesia, ropivacaine, epinephrine, postoperative pain management, skin perfusion


1. Introduction

   Outdated older medical literature stated that local anaesthetics with epinephrine should not be used
in acral areas. It indicates that epinephrine causes vasoconstriction via alpha-receptors in digital arteries
resulting in ischemia and gangrene [16]. However, supplementing a local anaesthetic with epinephrine

has a number of advantages in hand surgery: it may facilitate faster onset of anaesthesia, extend the effect
of anaesthetics with prolonged post procedure pain relief and reduce the amount required with resulting
to less toxicity [11, 27]. The addition of epinephrine speeds surgery by decreased bleeding into the
operative field and abandoned need for tourniquet use to control bleeding [8, 11]. Thus additional hazards
of tourniquet use such as nerve damage, increased oedema due to lymphatic obstruction, haematoma and
significant subsequent tissue acidosis with necrosis can be avoided [22].
   Several literature reviews [1, 4, 8, 9, 11, 17, 25, 26, 30, 31] of acral perfusion during anaesthetic
block with and without epinephrine additives have been published and have failed to show any support
behind the current dogma that the acral use of epinephrine in local anaesthetics causes necrosis. Cases
of digital gangrene after local anaesthesia were reported before 1950 and were associated with procaine
and cocaine injection with and without epinephrine. There are no reports of necrosis with commercial
lidocaine-epinephrine mixture. In recent years there are several current reports about the wide-awake
approach which is defined as the use of local anaesthetics e.g. vasoconstrictive lidocaine with epinephrine
in finger and hand surgery without use of a tourniquet [7, 18].
   In the view of the new developments in hand surgery with the above mentioned advantages, the aim
of this prospective, double-blinded, randomized study was to determine the exact chronological course
of changes in digit blood flow, post injection pain status and complication rates of volunteers without
operation using the digital two-injection dorsal technique block for local anesthesia of the finger nerve
bundles (Oberst’s-method anaesthetic block) with ropivacaine in different mixtures (group 1: ropivacaine
0.75%, group 2: ropivacaine 0.75% and epinephrine 1 : 1.000.000, group 3: local tumescent anaesthesia
0.15% TLA: 25 mL ropivacaine 1% (Naropin® ), 25 mL lidocaine 2% (Xylocain® ), 450 mL saline solution
plus epinephrine Suprarenin® 1 : 1.000.000). The results were compared to the outcomes after injection
of 2% lidocaine with 20 minutes tourniquet as this method is the commonly used method in hand surgery
(group 4).
   We hypothesized that the use of vasodilatative effecting ropivacaine with epinephrine additive allows
optimized skin perfusion rates with prolonged pain reduction and reduced risk of finger ischemia.


2. Patients and methods

2.1. Subjects

   In this prospective, double-blind, randomized study the acral perfusion of 20 healthy subjects (two
finger of each hand, in total 80 fingers), which are volunteers without any operation, were evaluated. The
study was approved by institutional review board and all subjects gave written informed consent. The
study was also performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the journal. Twelve volunteers
were female and eight male. Ages ranged from 21 to 60 years with an average age of 30.25 years (SD
9.9 years). Exclusion criterias were nicotine consumption, thromboangitis obliterans, diabetic disease,
peripheral artery occlusive disease, autoimmune dermatosis and other vascular diseases.

2.2. Methods

for possible exclusion criterias. Clinical evaluation included an Allen’s test in each volunteer to test for
collateral circulation to the hand by evaluating the patency of the radial and ulnar arteries. This test didn’t

2.4. Pain evaluation

  Pain was evaluated by provocation on the pulp with a needle. One physician tested all volunteers at
different time points (measuring points: 0, 16, 32, 48, 60, 360, 1400 minutes) using a visual pain scale
(VAS scale 1–10).

2.5. Statistical analysis

  Descriptive analysis and graphical presentation was performed using Diadem® Vs. 8.0 (National Instru-
ments, Austin, Texas) and Jmp® Vs. 3.1.5 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Mean, median, quartile and
simple standard deviation (SD) were calculated. A Student’s paired t test was used to determine whether
the measurement results for LDF and pain provocation prior to application of the substances changed
systematically after application. Significance level was set to p < 0.05.
  For a better overview of the results, the mean percentage of change in blood flow in relation to the
initial value before injection of one of the substances was calculated and documented. An overview of the
results of pain perception were given in form of box-and-whiskers plots showing averages, the highest
and lowest 95% confidence interval and extreme values.

4. Discussion

   Traditional medical education discourages the use of epinephrine for digital anaesthesia because of
possible epinephrine-induced vasoconstriction of end arteries and resulting ischemia and necrosis [16].
Interestingly in epinephrine supported studies it has become common practice to use an epinephrine
additive together with lidocaine [7, 18] even though lidocaine alone causes vasoconstriction in proportion
to the respective tissue concentration.

4.1. Perfusion evaluation

  In our study we determine the exact chronological course of changes in blood flow with ropivacaine
0.75% with different additives. After injection of ropivacaine 0.75% without additive the perfusion rates
showed an increasing vasodilatative effect for six hours. With epinephrine additive initial after injection
no significant perfusion changes were measured. Following the elimination of epinephrine after 6 hours
perfusion rates showed a vasodilatative and after 24 hours a reactive vasoconstrictive effect. Injection
of ropivacaine 0.75% plus potential vasoconstrictive additives (lidocaine, epinephrine 1 : 1.000.000) in
tumescent solution showed initially a reduced perfusion. Because of the longer effective period of ropiva-
caine 0.75% with an antagonistic vasodilatative effect perfusion values after 16 minutes trend to increase
continuous. Effects of ropivacaine on perfusion are described controversial in the literature [14], but

analysis of changes in acral blood flow showed vasodilatative effects and a long lasting effective period
(6–8 hours) [12, 28].
   Guinard et al. [10] showed an increased perfusion rate of 12.7% after injection of ropivacaine (0.25%) in
different skin areas of young pigs. Injection of ropivacaine 0.25% with epinephrine additive (1 : 200000)
decreased the perfusion rate of –60.4%.
   Cederholm et al. [3] concluded in their analysis that ropivacaine in different concentrations with
epinephrine additive (1 : 200.000) decrease the vasoconstrictive effect of epinephrine.
   Breuninger et al. [2] have described this advantage and beneficial use of ropivacaine in automatic local
tumescent anaesthesia in a total of 4,670 cutaneous surgical procedures of all kinds in 3,015 subjects.
   Based on the results of the present study as well as reports in the literature [3, 10, 11] the vasodilata-
tive effect of ropivacaine can compensate the vasoconstriction of epinephrine and other additives. This
increases the safety of epinephrine use in digits.

4.2. Analgesic efﬁcacy

   Our data showed the longest analgesic efficiency after injection in digits of 6 mL ropivacaine 0.75%
with epinephrine (18 hours) and tumescent solution (8 hours).
   Several studies with local anaesthetics and epinephrine additives [1, 8, 18, 21, 24] mention a faster
onset, decreased need for additional injections and prolonged post procedure pain relief, because of the
vasoconstrictive effect of epinephrine and resulting longer persistence of the local anaesthetics in the
tissue. The current study with first evaluated combination of vasodilatative ropivacaine, a long-acting
amide and vasoconstrictive epinephrine in different mixtures shows increased efficiency in acute pain
management and perfusion values with no risk of finger necrosis.
   Breuninger et al. [2] have described the use of ropivacaine and claimed that ropivacaine acted more
than twice as long as lidocaine. He stated ropivacaine as a reliable, long-acting local anaesthetic, which
reduce postoperative pain, make it easier to plan surgery and make it more often possible to avoid general
anaesthesia. Several other studies confirmed ropivacaine with epinephrine as an effective long-acting
local anaesthetic combination in lumbar epidural anaesthesia [15], femoral and sciatic nerve blocks [5]
and postoperative intraarticular injection after knee arthroplasty [20].
   Weber et al. [29] evaluated the effect of epinephrine on the duration of analgesia of 20 mL ropivacaine
0.5% or 0.2% injected in femoral three-in-one block for pain relief after total knee replacement. Their
results showed that epinephrine does not alter the duration of analgesia of the two solutions investigated.
   It seems that in fingers the combination of ropivacaine and epinephrine has prolonging effects with
advantages in hand surgery like resulting reduced bleeding and haematoma and improved acute pain
management.

4.3. Complications

   With regard to necrosis, nerve damage and risk of bleeding in the beginning of our study the perfusion
was lower with lidocaine and tourniquet than after injection of tumescent solution. In contrast to ropi-
vacaine 0.75% with and without additives, after opening of tourniquet the acral perfusion rate increased
suddenly with high values (+63.3%) and a presumed potentional higher risk of bleeding and haematoma
in the case of insufficient haemostasis.
   The commonly used method in finger surgery is an Oberst’s-method anaesthetic block combined with
the use of a tourniquet [7]. Tourniquets have been shown to cause nerve and muscle injury in animals

when pressures exceed 500 mmHg [30]. Digital tourniquets are well tolerated, provided that the skin is
anesthetized and pressures over 300 mmHg are avoided [23]. But because the tourniquet produces almost
total occlusion of the blood vessels the time of tourniquet use is limited. Generally it is accepted that
complete interruption of the perfusion in the acral areas can be used without damage for at least 1 hour
[6], after 2 hours reperfusion intervals are recommended [22]. After opening the tourniquets a distinct
hyper perfusion was observed [13].
  Data in the literature and our results suggest that the use of lidocaine and tourniquet has a higher risk
of post injection bleeding and haematoma in contrast to the use of ropivacaine and epinephrine.

4.4. Limitations

   Limitations of the study for clinical conclusions are failing perfusion measurements of subcutaneous
tissue. It can only supposed that changes of skin perfusion with different local anaesthetics and additives
have the same effect on deeper tissue with all advantages for surgical procedures. Furthermore sensibility
was not evaluated by a standard technique. We would advise the use for further studies.

4.5. Conclusions

  The benefits of using vasodilatative ropivacaine and epinephrine additive include:
  1. Compensation of the vasoconstrictive effect of epinephrine. This increases the safety of epinephrine
     use in digits and minimizes the risk of ischemia. Injection of ropivacaine 0.75% plus potential
     vasoconstrictive additives (lidocaine, epinephrine 1 : 1.000.000) in tumescent solution showed ini-
     tially a reduced skin perfusion with hypothetical clinical advantages like reduced blood loss and
     good view in the operation field. Because of the longer effective period of ropivacaine 0.75% with
     an antagonistic vasodilatative effect the risk of abrupt reactive hyper perfusion decreased and the
     postoperative pain management is improved.
  2. More efficient and prolonged anesthesia and local inhibition of perfusion with good visualisation
     of the operation field and reduced bleeding and haematoma. Of course, in cases like Dupuytren’s
     contracture longer pain relief postoperatively is not necessarily desired because the sensibility cannot
     be examined. There, the advantages of ropivacaine are limited.
  3. Possibly even decreased need for a tourniquet with reduced risk of tourniquet associated tissue
     damage or bleeding/haematoma caused by vasodilatation and perfusion rise after opening the
     tourniquet.
