ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

Introduction: Guided plasticity, induced by cutaneous forearm anaesthesia, improves hand sensibility in
patients with nerve injury and vibration-induced neuropathy. This study investigated whether patients
with carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) would benefit from cutaneous forearm anaesthesia.
Methods: Seventy patients with clinical and electroneurography-verified CTS were randomised to sensory training combined with either an anaesthetic cream (EMLAV) (n ¼ 34) or a placebo cream (n ¼ 36)
on the volar part of the forearm. The treatment was repeated at increasing intervals over 8 weeks. The
primary outcome was the Boston carpal tunnel questionnaire (BCTQ) symptom severity scale after 8
weeks. Secondary outcomes included activity limitations, sensory function, and nerve conduction. This
study compared the longitudinal changes between the two groups, and with correction for multiple
comparisons it also compared the longitudinal change within the groups.
Results: There were no significant differences in primary or secondary outcomes between the groups.
However, the BCTQ symptom severity scale improved significantly within the EMLAV group over the 8week period (p ¼ 0.001). Apart from this, no significant improvements in activity limitations, sensory
function, or nerve conduction were seen in the two groups compared to baseline. Altogether, 47% of
patients in the EMLAV group and 61% in the placebo group had been operated on with carpal tunnel
release by 12 months.
Conclusion: An 8-week treatment protocol with cutaneous forearm anaesthesia to guide brain plasticity
gave no significant subjective or objective improvements in hand function compared to placebo.

Introduction
Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), where the median nerve is
compressed at the wrist, is the most common compression
neuropathy; it affects 4% of women and 2% of men at
some stage in life [1,2]. Patients experience discomfort and
often pain in the hand, as well as impaired hand function [3].
CTS can be treated conservatively with wrist splints and activity modifications [4,5], but many patients have persistent
symptoms and are operated on with carpal tunnel release.
Around 13 000 patients undergo surgery for CTS each year in
Sweden, which has 9 million inhabitants (data derived from
the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare).
Traditionally, surgery has been considered to relieve symptoms, but some previous studies have indicated that a number of patients operated on with carpal tunnel release have a
poor outcome in terms of relief of symptoms [5,6].
The brain is constantly changing according to the pattern
of afferent nerve impulses. A changed afferent nerve signal
pattern from the hand, as seen in patients with traumatic
nerve injuries and some neuropathies, results in changes in
the somatosensory areas of the brain [7,8]. This dynamic
capacity of the brain, i.e. plasticity, provides new possibilities,
as plasticity can be guided to substitute for functions that

have been damaged or lost [9]. One example of guided
plasticity is temporary cutaneous anaesthesia of the forearm,
which has been shown to improve sensibility in the hand in
healthy volunteers, in patients operated on for median or
ulnar nerve transections, and in patients with neuropathy
due to long-term exposure to hand-held vibrating tools
[10–12]. After treating the forearm with the anaesthetic
cream EMLAV, no nerve signals are sent from the anaesthetised area to the brain. The neurons in the primary somatosensory area that usually respond to sensory information
from the forearm start responding to sensory information
from the hand; thus, more nerve cells are available to process information from cutaneous receptors in the hand
[11,13]. The improved hand function remains for several
hours after a single treatment [11]. Repeated sessions with
cutaneous anaesthesia at gradually increasing intervals have
been shown to give lasting improvement in patients who
have been operated on for median or ulnar nerve transections [10] and in patients with vibration-induced neuropathy
[12]. Using a randomised, placebo controlled study design,
we investigated whether patients with carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) would benefit from an 8-week treatment protocol involving guided plasticity in the form of cutaneous
forearm anaesthesia.

After completing the 8-week treatment, all participants were
offered a surgical decompression of the median nerve.

Randomisation
All eligible patients received standardised information about
the study both orally and in writing. After inclusion, they
were randomly assigned to one of two interventions according to a computer-generated randomisation list, created using
the ‘randomise’ function in Microsoft ExcelV.

Sample size
The primary outcome was the symptom severity scale (SSS)
from the Boston carpal tunnel syndrome questionnaire
(BCTQ) [17]. According to the sample size estimation for SSS
(90% power and 5% significance level), the trial would be
able to detect a minimum difference of 0.8 (SD 1.0) in the
SSS [14,15] between the EMLAV group and the placebo group
when 66 patients were randomly assigned.

in the study. The inclusion criteria were: subjective symptoms
for more than 3 months, clinical signs of CTS [3], an electroneurography (ENeG), with a fractionated sensory nerve conduction speed over the wrist of 40 m/s or less. The exclusion

patient-rated outcome measures (PROMs).
Seventy consecutive patients, 16 men and 54 women, with

all the patients and ascertained the clinical diagnosis. A medical history was taken, focusing on adverse effects from previous use of local anaesthetic agents, including allergic
reactions and current medication, and concentrating especially on medications that could interact with local anaesthetic agents.

Outcomes
To ensure blinding of the investigators performing direct
assessments, application and subsequent removal of EMLAV
or placebo was performed by the same researcher (MF), who
randomised and informed the participants in a standardised
manner about treatment and follow-up. MF also instructed
the study patients not to comment on the treatment to the
investigator performing the direct assessments. Two therapists – who were blind regarding the treatment and who
were experienced in assessment of nerve function – carried
out all the direct assessments and administered the PROMs.
All electrophysiological examinations were performed by
the same technician and were evaluated independently by
the same neurophysiologist.

The SSS from the Boston carpal tunnel syndrome questionnaire (BCTQ) [18] was used for primary outcome of symptoms
at baseline and after 8 weeks of treatment.
Secondary outcome
Activity limitations were assessed with the short version of
the ‘disability of arm, shoulder and hand questionnaire’
(QuickDASH) [16] at baseline and after 8 weeks of treatment.

Direct assessments focusing mainly on sensory functions
were performed at baseline, directly after the first treatment,
and at the 8-week follow-up. ENeG was performed at baseline
and after 8 weeks. During sensory testing, vision was
occluded with a screen and the hand being tested was resting comfortably in a supine position.
Assessment of cutaneous touch/pressure thresholds was
done on the tip of the second digit using a set of 20
Semmes-Weinstein monofilaments (SWM) (North Coast
Medical Inc., Gilroy, CA) [20]. Assessment was started with
SWM no. 2.83 (representing a pressure of 70 mg) and thereafter in an ascending or descending order depending on the
answer for the first filament. Each filament was applied three
times according to a standardised procedure [20]. Results
were quantified from 0–20, representing the 20 monofilaments, with 20 corresponding to the lowest threshold.
Two-point discrimination (2PD) was used at fingertip level
of the second digit for assessment of tactile discrimination
(tactile gnosis). The instrument used for 2PD was a single
blunted stainless steel pin, and pairs of them, with a diameter
of 300 lm, were mounted on two separate discs to allow
easy switching between the distances. Distances between the
pins of 0.7, 1.0, 1.3, 1.6, 1.9, 2.2, 2.5, 2.8, 3.1, 3.4, 4.0, 4.3, 4.6,
and 5.0 mm were used. 2PD testing was done according to

the ‘Moberg Method’ in descending order, with sufficient
pressure to cause skin blanching. A score of at least seven
correct answers out of 10 was required to permit testing with
pins with a smaller gap between them.
To familiarise the test subject with the test procedure and
to eliminate the possibility of biased results due to learning
effects, tests of 2PD and touch threshold on the third digit
were performed before the baseline testing
Sensory electro-neurography was performed bilaterally
using Nicolet Viking SelectV equipment (Nicolet Biomedical
Inc., Madison, WI). The patient’s skin temperature was kept
above 30  C during the examination. Sensory fibres were
stimulated in the thumb, the index finger, and the long finger
for the median nerve. Ring electrodes were placed at the
proximal interphalangeal and distal interphalangeal joints for
the index and long fingers, and for the thumb, just proximal,
and distal to the interphalangeal joint. Recording electrodes
were placed over the median nerve at the proximal wrist
crease. Amplitudes and conduction velocities were measured.

All participants were checked 12 months after inclusion, and
asked if they had been operated on with carpal tunnel
release on the side that had been treated with EMLAV or
placebo.

Statistical analysis
IBM SPSS Statistics (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences,
version 22 for Mac; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used for the
statistical assessment of data obtained from the testing.
Results for each group are presented as mean and SD. We
used t-test, and differences within and between groups are
presented as means with 95% confidence interval (CI). Effect
sizes of the changes in symptoms and specific sensory functions were also calculated. Any p-values below 0.05 were considered significant.
The longitudinal changes between baseline results and
results after 8 weeks of treatment were calculated within
groups. Due to multiple pairwise comparisons, any p-values
below 0.004 after Bonferroni correction were considered
significant.

Results
No treatment-related adverse events were observed in any of
the groups. Comparisons by t-test at baseline did not show
any significant differences between the groups in any of the
outcome parameters (Table 2).

Primary outcome
There was no significant difference in the BCTQ symptom
severity scale between the EMLAV group and the placebo
group when comparing baseline and 8 weeks (p ¼ 0.78, mean
difference ¼ 0.05 units, 95% CI ¼ 0.3–0.4).
R

Secondary outcome
Mean patient satisfaction after 8 weeks, estimated on a visual
analogue scale, was 53 (SD ¼ 28) in the EMLAV group and 40
(SD ¼ 28) in the placebo group. The difference between
groups was not statistically significant, however.
Furthermore, there were no significant differences
between the EMLAV group and the placebo group when
comparing changes in outcome between baseline and 8
weeks, regarding QuickDASH, SWM, and 2PD. After 8 weeks
of treatment, no significant improvements were seen in the
direct assessments of sensory functions in the two groups
compared to baseline.
The BCTQ symptom severity scale had improved significantly in the EMLAV group after 8 weeks (p ¼ 0.001).
R

R

R

However, in absolute numbers the improvement was 0.5 units
in the BCTQ symptom severity scale that goes from 1–5,
where 0.8 units is considered a clinically important change
[15]. Thus, the improvement may be of limited clinical importance (Table 2).
ENeG did not show any significant differences after 8
weeks, either within groups or between the two groups
(Table 2). Despite this, 1 year after inclusion in the study, 15
of the 32 patients in the EMLAV group (47%) and 22 of the
36 patients in the placebo group (61%) had chosen a surgical
intervention for their CTS.
R

Discussion
No significant difference in severity of symptoms according
to the BCTQ symptom severity scale was seen between the
two groups after 8 weeks of treatment. However, a withingroup analysis showed that cutaneous forearm anaesthesia
with EMLAV in combination with sensory re-education
resulted in significantly reduced symptoms, although it was at
a level of limited clinical importance.
Following forearm deafferentation, more nerve cells in the
primary somatosensory cortex are allocated to process sensory information from the hand. This has resulted in improved
sensibility in the hand in healthy volunteers [13] and in
patients with median or ulnar nerve injuries or neuropathy
due to long-term exposure to hand-held vibrating tools [8,10].
In the present study, patients treated with EMLAV showed an
improvement in sensibility after 90 minutes, although this was
not significant after correction for multiple comparisons
(p ¼ 0.02). This indicates that the concept of cutaneous
deafferentation also has the potential to work in patients
with CTS (Table 2). However, repeated treatment with EMLAV
did not consolidate the initial improvement in sensibility.
Interestingly, even though there was no improvement in perception of touch or tactile discrimination after 8 weeks of
treatment, patients in the EMLAV group experienced a subjective reduction in symptoms. This indicates that, although
EMLAV treatment cannot improve sensibility, the combination
of EMLAV, sensory training, and a general awareness of the
use of the hand can reduce the symptoms. A reduction of
symptoms in the BCTQ symptom severity scale was also seen
in the placebo group after 8 weeks, but this difference was
not significant. This might be the reason for the lack of a
group difference at 8 weeks.

There are many possible explanations for these results. The
nerve injury behind the symptoms in CTS is probably different from that in both patients operated on due to a median
or ulnar nerve transection and patients with neuropathy due
to long-term exposure to hand-held vibrating tools, where
treatment with cutaneous forearm deafferentation has proven
successful [7]. In patients with a nerve transection, there is an
incomplete peripheral re-innervation, and, secondary to this, a
cerebral reorganisation. In patients with neuropathy due to
long-term exposure to hand-held vibrating tools, peripheral
nerve structures, fibres, and receptors are damaged – and,
because of this, the sensory areas in the brain reorganise [8].
In both of these patient groups, sensory functions in the
hand are reduced, as demonstrated by a decreased tactile
discrimination and perception of touch. In patients with CTS,
the pathology is a compression of the median nerve at the
wrist, resulting in a reduced speed of the nerve signal [21],
but most patients have normal sensory functions in the hand
when assessed with 2PD and SWM. A reduced sensory function of the hand is considered to be a very late sign, indicating severe compression of the median nerve [22]. It is
thought that the improved function after cutaneous anaesthesia is based on more nerve cells being available in the
brain for processing of sensory information from the hand. It
is possible that the sensory information sent in the median
nerve in patients with CTS but normal sensibility in the hand
cannot be processed better when more nerve cells are
recruited, so we cannot detect a permanent improvement
after 8 weeks of treatment. On the other hand, it is possible
that patients with a very severe compression, detected as a
marked decrease in nerve conduction speed or a complete
conduction block (resulting in decreased sensibility in the
hand) might possibly improve after EMLAV treatment.
However, there were few such patients in the present study,
as we excluded patients with a complete conduction block,
and the majority of participants had a moderate reduction in
conduction velocity with a median speed of 31 m/s (interquartile range ¼ 28–35 m/s) over the carpal tunnel.
Importantly, patients with prolonged severe compression are
known to have permanent injury of the median nerve [22,23].
To treat such patients with EMLAV could possibly improve
sensibility, but the compression of the median nerve is not
relieved – so the risk of permanent nerve injury remains,
making EMLAV treatment unadvisable in such cases.
The sensory assessments used here could not capture the
relief of symptoms experienced by the patients in the EMLAV
group. This could indicate that the sensitivity of the test
instruments was too low. The Semmes-Weinstein monofilaments have proven to have good responsiveness in a previous longitudinal study after surgical intervention in CTS [24].
All the patients in our study had nerve conduction below 41
m/s and clinical signs of CTS, but they had superior sensory
function at baseline compared to what has previously been
reported in patients with CTS [24]. Only nine of the 70 participants had abnormal touch threshold (SWM above filament
no. 2.83) at baseline, and only two had 2PD over 5 mm at
baseline. This skewed distribution of the data with a ceiling
effect, with very few study participants below cut-off for normality in both touch thresholds and discriminative touch at
R

5

baseline (Table 2), means that the changes after 8 weeks of
treatment could only be very small. Results from the PROMs;
QuickDASH and BCTQ symptom severity scale had a wider
distribution. At baseline, QuickDASH had a mean of 30 and
ranged from 0–77 in the EMLAV group, and there was a
mean of 35 (ranging from 0–93) in the placebo group. This
indicates that some patients had major problems regarding
not only sensory functions, but also performance of daily
activities.
This study had some shortcomings. Although CTS is relatively common, it was difficult to recruit patients. One possible explanation for this is that many patients know that an
operation often alleviates symptoms. The waiting time for surgery is very short, so many of them would have preferred to
choose an operation.
Some patients experienced benefits from the treatment,
and general satisfaction with the treatment was higher,
although not significantly so, among those treated with
EMLAV rather than placebo. This is also illustrated by the fact
that 47% in the EMLAV group as compared to 61% in the placebo group had been operated on within 1 year after treatment. Thus, EMLAV treatment might be an alternative for
some patients. Furthermore, EMLAV treatment could be of
interest to patients who have already been operated on with
carpal tunnel release, but for whom the operation has not
relieved the symptoms. In such patients, the persistent symptoms may be due to many different causes – many of which
involve a disturbed afferent signal pattern from the hand to
the brain. In such cases, recruitment of more neurons in the
somatosensory areas through cutaneous anaesthesia might
possibly improve the symptoms. Further studies are needed to
identify which patients with CTS may benefit from this treatment. Also, the optimal treatment protocol in terms of timing
and duration of treatment would have to be decided on.
