a b s t r a c t
Epinephrine (EPI) contributes to hyperalgesia in inflammatory and stress conditions. EPI signals via
adrenoceptors, which are regulated by G protein-coupled receptor kinase 2 (GRK2). We previously
reported that GRK2 is decreased in nociceptors during chronic inflammation. Herein, we investigated
whether GRK2 modulates EPI-induced mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia by using GRK2+/ mice,
which express 50% of the GRK2 protein. We demonstrate for the first time that EPI-induced mechanical
as well as thermal hyperalgesia is prolonged to approximately 21 days in GRK2+/ mice, whereas it lasts
only 3 to 4 days in wild-type mice. Using cell- specific GRK2-deficient mice, we further show that a low
level of GRK2 in primary sensory neurons is critical for this prolongation of EPI-induced hyperalgesia.
Low GRK2 in microglia had only a small effect on EPI-induced hyperalgesia. Low GRK2 in astrocytes
did not alter EPI-induced hyperalgesia. EPI-induced hyperalgesia was prolonged similarly in mice with
tamoxifen-induced homozygous or heterozygous deletion of GRK2. In terms of EPI signalling pathways,
the protein kinase A (PKA) inhibitor H-89 inhibited EPI-induced mechanical hyperalgesia in wild-type
mice, whereas H-89 had no effect in mice with low GRK2 in sensory neurons (SNS-GRK2+/ mice). Conversely, intraplantar injection of the protein kinase Ce PKCe inhibitor TAT-PKCev1-2 inhibited hyperalgesia
in sensory neuron specific (SNS)-GRK2+/ mice and not in wild-type mice. These results indicate that low
GRK2 in primary sensory neurons switches EPI-induced signalling from a protein kinase A-dependent
toward a PKCe-dependent pathway that ultimately mediates prolonged EPI-induced hyperalgesia.

1. Introduction
The sympathoadrenal system contributes to several forms of
pain, including inflammatory pain, stress-induced pain, and neuropathic pain, via secretion of catecholamines such as epinephrine
(EPI) and norepinephrine [15,17,28]. Moreover, local administration of EPI induces transient thermal and mechanical hyperalgesia
[18]. EPI exerts its effects by high affinity binding to b1- and
b2-adrenergic receptors (ARs), and by lower affinity binding to aARs. These receptors belong to the family of G protein-coupled
receptors (GPCRs). Signalling via these GPCRs is regulated by a

In general, activation of b2-AR by EPI increases intracellular levels of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) via activation of a
heterotrimeric G protein of the Gs subclass. This increase in cAMP
activates PKA to induce a cellular response [3]. In addition, b2-AR
activation leads to signalling to mitogen-activated protein kinase
kinase (MEK)/ERK via coupling to Gi and/or via the scaffold function of b-arrestins [11]. Moreover, cAMP activates not only PKA
but also Epac, which in turn activates PKCe [1,6,12].
The aim of the present study is to investigate the impact of low
endogenous GRK2 in sensory neurons on mechanical and thermal
hyperalgesia induced by the stress hormone EPI.

2.3. Mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia
Before the experiments, mice were exposed to the equipment
without any nociceptive stimulation for 1 to 2 hours per day for
2 days. On the third day, mice were placed in the test environment
15 to 20 minutes before testing. Baseline responses were determined on 3 days before intraplantar injection of EPI. Mechanical
hyperalgesia was measured using von Frey hairs as previously described [34]. The 50% paw withdrawal threshold was calculated
using the up-and-down method [2]. In short, animals were placed
on a wire grid bottom through which the von Frey hairs were applied (bending force range from 0.02 to 1.4 g, starting with 0.16 g;
Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL, USA). The hair force was increased or decreased according to the response. Clear paw withdrawal, shaking,
or licking were considered as nociceptive-like responses. Ambulation was considered an ambiguous response, and in such cases the
stimulus was repeated.
Heat withdrawal latency times were determined using the Hargreaves (IITC Life Science; Woodland Hills, CA, USA) test as described previously [9]. Intensity of the light beam was chosen to
induce a heat withdrawal latency time of approximately 8 seconds
at baseline.

3. Results
3.1. EPI-induced mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia in
GRK2+/ mice
To investigate whether GRK2 regulates EPI-induced mechanical
hyperalgesia, we measured 50% paw withdrawal thresholds in WT
and GRK2+/ mice in response to intraplantar injection of EPI.
Mechanical thresholds at baseline were similar in female and
male WT and GRK2+/ mice (females: 0.86 ± 0.06 g vs 0.82 ±
0.09 g; n = 8; males: 0.99 ± 0.04 g vs 0.96 ± 0.04 g; n = 8). In
GRK2+/ mice, EPI-induced hyperalgesia lasted approximately
21 days vs 3 to 4 days in WT mice (Fig. 1A and B). EPI-induced
mechanical hyperalgesia was prolonged to a similar extent in
female and male GRK2+/ mice (Fig. 1A and B). At a low dose of
EPI (3 ng/paw), mechanical hyperalgesia also lasted longer in

GRK2+/ than in WT mice (Fig. 1C). In addition, at this low dose
of EPI, acute hyperalgesia was increased in GRK2+/ mice.
EPI-induced thermal hyperalgesia also differed between WT
and GRK2+/ mice. In male and female WT mice, EPI-induced thermal hyperalgesia lasted 3 to 4 days. However, in GRK2+/ mice,
thermal hyperalgesia was of markedly longer duration (nearly
21 days) (Fig. 2A and B). Consistent with our previous reports
[5,6,34], baseline heat withdrawal latencies were similar in WT
and GRK2+/ mice (female: 7.90 ± 0.15 seconds vs 7.97 ± 0.18 seconds; male: 8.47 ± 0.09 seconds vs 8.35 ± 0.10 seconds; n = 8).
The effect of reduced GRK2 on hyperalgesia was similar in male
and female mice. Our earlier experiments on the contribution of
GRK2 to hyperalgesia were all performed in female mice [5,6,34].
Therefore, we used only female mice for all subsequent
experiments.

3.5. Role of microglial/macrophage and astrocyte GRK2 in EPI-induced
hyperalgesia
To assess the contribution of low GRK2 in microglia/macrophages
to the prolongation of EPI-induced hyperalgesia, we compared
heterozygous LysM-GRK2+/ mice with control LysM-GRK2+/+
littermates. LysM-GRK2+/ mice have a reduced level of GRK2 only
in microglia/macrophages/granulocytes [5]. EPI-induced mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia were prolonged by 2 to 4 days in
LysM-GRK2+/ mice compared with control LysM-GRK2+/+ littermates. However, EPI-induced hyperalgesia in LysM-GRK2+/ mice
lasted significantly shorter than in GRK2+/ mice with low GRK2
in all cells (Fig. 6A and B as compared with Fig. 1). Baseline 50%
paw withdrawal thresholds (0.85 ± 0.02 g vs 0.89 ± 0.02 g; n = 8/
group) and heat withdrawal latency times (7.94 ± 0.11 seconds vs
8.25 ± 0.13 seconds; n = 8/group) were similar between LysMGRK2+/ mice and control littermates.
To assess the contribution of low GRK2 in astrocytes, we measured EPI-induced hyperalgesia in GFAP-GRK2+/ mice and control
littermates. The data in Fig. 7 show that EPI-induced mechanical
hyperalgesia in GFAP-GRK2+/ mice did not differ from control
littermates.
3.6. DRG structure
To examine the effects of GRK2 deletion on gross morphology of
the DRG, we compared the distribution of different subtypes of
DRG neurons in SNS-GRK2+/ and WT mice. We did not observe
any genotype-related difference in the percentage of IB4+ and
IB4- small-diameter neurons that are thought to be important for
inflammatory hyperalgesia. In addition, there were no differences
in the size distribution of DRG neurons between genotypes (Fig. 8).

3.7. Effect of low GRK2 EPI-induced hyperalgesic signalling
Next we investigated whether a decrease in nociceptor GRK2
led to a change in the use of signalling pathways that mediate
EPI-induced hyperalgesia. In WT mice, the PKA inhibitor H-89 significantly reduced EPI-induced mechanical hyperalgesia (Fig. 9A).
However, in SNS-GRK2+/ mice, H-89 had no effect at all on EPI-induced mechanical hyperalgesia (Fig. 9B). Conversely, pretreatment
with the PKCe inhibitor TAT-PKCev1-2 had no detectable effect on
EPI-induced mechanical hyperalgesia in WT mice (Fig. 9C),
whereas inhibition of PKCe in SNS-GRK2+/ mice markedly reduced
EPI-induced mechanical hyperalgesia (Fig. 9D). Notably, the severity and duration of EPI-induced hyperalgesia in TAT-PKCev1-2-treated SNS-GRK2+/ mice was similar to that of WT mice.
The MEK inhibitor U0126 partially inhibited the acute phase of
EPI-induced mechanical hyperalgesia in both WT and SNS-GRK2+/
mice and entirely prevented the late phase of EPI-induced mechanical hyperalgesia in SNS-GRK2+/ mice (Fig. 9E and F).

In our earlier studies we had shown that the level of GRK2 in

that a reduced level of GRK2 in microglia/macrophages has only a
small effect on EPI-induced mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia.
There is another discrepancy between the present results and
those obtained in the model of PGE2-induced hyperalgesia. In this
last model, a reduction in nociceptor GRK2 only was not sufficient
to fully explain the more pronounced prolongation of PGE2-hyperalgesia in total GRK2+/ mice [6]. These findings imply that low
GRK2 in other cells, e.g., microglia/macrophages contributes to
the longer duration of the hyperalgesic response to PGE2 in mice
with low GRK2 in all cells. In the present study, EPI-induced
mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia was prolonged to the same
extent in SNS-GRK2+/ mice as compared with GRK2+/ mice.

Therefore, we propose that low GRK2 in nociceptors alone is sufficient to obtain the maximal effect of low GRK2 on EPI-induced
hyperalgesia, whereas low GRK2 in both nociceptors and in
microglia/macrophages is required to maximally prolong PGE2
hyperalgesia. GRK2 in astrocytes had no effect on regulating hyperalgesia in response to all stimuli we tested so far, including EPI.

receptors increases intracellular cAMP while CCL3 and IL-1b receptors signal via cAMP-independent pathways [20,25,35], we suggest
that neuronal GRK2 orchestrates the duration of inflammatory
hyperalgesia only when the inflammatory stimulus involves a
cAMP signalling component.

To investigate whether the effect of low GRK2 on EPI hyperalgesia is dependent on other more functional developmental effects,
we used tamoxifen-inducible GRK2 / and GRK2+/ mice. The results of these experiments do not point toward a contribution of
developmental effects of low GRK2 to hyperalgesia; EPI-induced
hyperalgesia was similarly enhanced and prolonged in mice with
low GRK2 in all cells during the entire life span versus mice in
which the reduction in GRK2 in all cells was induced in adult life.
Interestingly, we observed a lack of gene dosage effect when comparing the hyperalgesic response to EPI in tamoxifen-inducible
GRK2 / and GRK2+/ mice, suggesting that a 50% decrease of
GRK2 is already sufficient to induce a maximal effect on the prolongation of EPI-induced hyperalgesia.
In terms of signalling pathways, we showed here that EPI-induced hyperalgesia in WT mice was blocked by the PKA inhibitor
H-89. However, in SNS-GRK2+/ mice, we did not observe any effect of the PKA inhibitor on EPI-induced mechanical hyperalgesia.
Conversely, inhibition of PKCe did not affect EPI-induced hyperalgesia in female WT mice, whereas it inhibited EPI-hyperalgesia in
SNS-GRK2+/ mice. These results are in accordance with the mechanism we recently proposed for regulation of PGE2-induced thermal hyperalgesia by low nociceptor GRK2 [6]. Thus, our present
findings confirm and expand further the emerging scenario that
low nociceptor GRK2 induces a switch in cAMP signalling from
PKA- toward PKCe-dependent pathways.
Hucho et al. have described that cAMP signalling to PKCe
involves Epac [12]. We have shown that in vitro low GRK2 causes
biased cAMP signalling to Epac and its downstream target Rap1 [6].
We also showed that GRK2 and Epac1 are present in the same protein complex, and we proposed that binding of GRK2 to Epac inhibits activation of Epac in response to cAMP-inducing agents. This
interpretation is supported by our finding that in vitro, Rap1 activation by the Epac activator 8p-CPT is stronger in GRK2+/ DRG
neurons as compared with WT DRG neurons [6]. On the basis of
all of these converging data, we propose that low GRK2 in nociceptors determines neuronal responsiveness to cAMP-inducing agents
by shifting the balance from PKA to PKCe via a mechanism involving increased Epac activation when GRK2 is low.
The signalling routes involved in EPI-induced mechanical
hyperalgesia as analysed by the Randall-Selito test in rats have
been described before by Dina et al. [4]. These investigators
showed that female rats do not use PKA but only MEK signalling
to establish EPI-induced mechanical hyperalgesia [4]. Our results
indicate that in WT female mice EPI-induced mechanical hyperalgesia is mediated by both PKA and MEK/ERK. These apparent differences in the use of PKA may be due to species differences (rat
versus mouse), the way mechanical threshold was analysed (von
Frey versus Randall-Selito test), and/or the use of different PKA
inhibitors (H89 versus the peptide inhibitor WIPTIDE).
In mice with low neuronal GRK2, the prolonged hyperalgesia is
no longer dependent on PKA but is routed via both PKCe and MEK/
ERK-dependent pathways because blocking of either of these pathways prevented the prolongation of hyperalgesia. We therefore
propose that the EPI-induced activation of the MEK/ERK pathway
predominates in conditions of both normal and low GRK2. Low
nociceptor GRK2 biases EPI signalling from PKA to PKCe, but activity of both the MEK/ERK and the PKCe pathway is required for
development of prolonged hyperalgesia in mice with low nociceptor GRK2. Whether in conditions of low GRK2 MEK/ERK becomes
part of the signalling complex with (Epac-)PKCe or MEK/ERK activation occurs as part of a separate signalling route remains to be
established.
The fact that inflammation reduces GRK2 in both DRGs and
spinal cord to the same extent as in the heterozygous knockout

mice [6,34] underlines the translational significance of our current
data and extends the emerging concept that GRK2 is a pivotal molecule in the transition from acute to prolonged hyperalgesia in disease processes comprising inflammation and/or chronic stress.
