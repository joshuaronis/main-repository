Eutectic mixture of local anesthetics and amethocaine as
topical anesthetics in pediatrics: a meta-analysis

BACKGROUND: Pediatric patients often receive topical anesthesia before skin procedures in the Emergency Department, with
EMLA cream and amethocaine gel being common choices. The most effective option remains a subject of debate.
OBJECTIVE: Our goal was to compare EMLA cream with amethocaine gel in pediatric patients undergoing topical anesthesia,
focusing on outcomes: first-attempt cannulation success, child-reported visual analogue scale (VAS) score, parent-reported VAS
score, observed pain score, child-reported absence of pain, and child-reported acceptable anesthesia.
METHODS: A database search for studies comparing EMLA cream and amethocaine gel in pediatric topical anesthesia was
conducted. Two reviewers extracted and cross-verified data, with a third ensuring accuracy. Using R software, a pairwise metaanalysis was performed via the Mantel-Haenszel method. Outcomes were pooled as risk ratios or standard mean differences with
95% confidence intervals using the random-effects model.
RESULTS: Amethocaine gel surpasses EMLA cream in child-reported pain absence and first cannulation success. No significant
differences were found in child-reported acceptable anesthesia or observed pain scores. Similarly, child- and parent-reported VAS
scores showed no variations between EMLA and amethocaine.
CONCLUSION: This analysis favors amethocaine gel for pediatric topical anesthesia. Further large randomized trials comparing
EMLA cream and amethocaine gel in pediatric patients are warranted.
Pediatric Research (2024) 96:51–56; https://doi.org/10.1038/s41390-024-03113-7
IMPACT: Procedural pain is a major concern for pediatric patients, their families, and physicians. Topical anesthesia is routinely
given prior to children undergoing skin-related procedures in the Emergency Department. In pediatric patients, topical anesthetics
such as eutectic mixture of local anesthetics cream and amethocaine gel have proved to be pioneering in pain reduction, but the
most effective method is often disputed. Presently, this is the most comprehensive pooled analysis of trials comparing EMLA cream
and amethocaine gel in pediatric patients undergoing topical anesthesia. Amethocaine performed better with regards to childreported absence of pain and first attempt cannulation success.

INTRODUCTION
Skin-related procedures like cannulation, venepuncture, or catheter insertion can be distressing for pediatric patients, parents, and
providers due to the associated pain. For this reason, percutaneous local anesthesia prior to the procedure has become a
mainstay of treatment within the pediatric population. Within
acute clinical settings like the Emergency Department (ED), topical
anesthetic creams have been shown to reduce the pain and
discomfort associated with such procedures.1
Eutectic mixture of local anesthetics (EMLA) cream (lidocaineprilocaine 5% cream) was the first effective formulation of an
anesthetic for skin use and proved a breakthrough in pain relief
for pediatric patients in initial clinical trials.2–9 A minimum
application time of 60 min is required for EMLA.3 Due to the
possibility of methemoglobinemia induced by prilocaine metabolites, EMLA is contraindicated in its use on diseased skin and in

very young infants.10 EMLA can also cause blanching of the skin,
which can complicate intravenous catheter insertion.11,12
Amethocaine (tetracaine), a newer commercially available gel
formulation, is more lipophilic than EMLA and has been proven to
be effective after only 20–30 min of application.13–15 Additionally,
studies have shown that amethocaine may be associated with a
lower risk of methemoglobinemia, greater anesthetic potency,
and a longer duration of action than EMLA.14,16–18 Aside from
erythema, amethocaine has also been shown to cause vasodilation, which improves success rates for intravenous catheter
insertions on the first attempt.12,18 EMLA application, on the other
hand, produces a biphasic vasoactive response dominated by
vasoconstriction.19 There is no clear agreement on the best topical
anesthetic mixture, as each one has its own benefits and
drawbacks. The choice often varies based on the preferences of
the institution and the operator. Thus, EMLA cream was compared

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study design/Guidelines
This study was designed and conducted in accordance with the
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and MetaAnalyses (PRISMA) and Meta-Analysis of Observational Studies in
Epidemiology (MOOSE)20 guidelines (Supplementary Fig. 1).
Comprehensive search strategy
The PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane Library libraries were
comprehensively searched without restriction from inception to
identify randomized control trials (RCTs) and comparative cohort
studies comparing EMLA cream and amethocaine gel in pediatric
patients undergoing topical anesthesia. The review encompassed
trials that involved children and youth under 18 years old, of any
sex, ethnicity, and for any skin-related procedure. The full search
strategy is available in Supplementary Table 1 and included the
terms ‘EMLA’, ‘eutectic mixture of local anesthetics’, ‘lidocaineprilocaine 5% cream’, ‘amethocaine gel’ and ‘tetracaine gel.’
Single-arm or non-comparative studies, commentaries, review
articles, conference presentations, and animal studies were
excluded from this review. Additionally, relevant references of
included studies were searched for eligibility.
Study selection/Data extraction
After conducting a search of primary literature, a total of 1471
studies were identified and gathered for further analysis. Two
authors (K.V. & A.A.) reviewed 1438 titles and abstracts after
eliminating duplicate citations. Next, two investigators (A.A. &
D.M.) reviewed the full texts of the remaining articles for
eligibility, with disagreements being resolved by a third
investigator (P.F.). Further details of the screening process and
reasons for article exclusion are available in Supplementary
Fig. 2.
After reviewing the articles, two authors (K.V. and A.A.)
independently extracted data from the studies that were
included. If there were any disagreements, a third author
(M.A.) reviewed them. The following data were collected and
tabulated: investigators’ names, country of institution, year of
publication, number of patients, (Table 1), baseline demographics of included patients [mean age, percent of males,
percent of females, mean weight, and venepunctures in previous
12 months], intraoperative characteristics [erythema at site of
application, location of cannula application (hand or antecubital), redness at site of application, blanching at site of
application, tubes of cream used, the average weight of cream
used, number of attempts at cannulation, time to venous
cannulation, application time] (Supplementary Table 2), and
post-operative outcomes [first attempt cannulation success rate,
child-reported visual analogue scale (VAS) score, parentreported visual analogue scale (VAS) score, observed pain score,
child-reported absence of pain, and child-reported acceptable
anesthesia] (Figs. 1–6).
Study quality assessment
To ensure accuracy and fairness, two separate authors (A.A. &
D.M.) evaluated the quality of each study using the Newcastle
Ottawa Scale21 for observational studies, which rates observational
studies quality via asterisk score in three domains: up to four
asterisks for patient selection, up to two for comparability of study
arms, and up to three asterisks for sufficient outcome follow-up
duration/assessment. (Supplementary Table 3). In cases where
there were discrepancies, a third author (P.F.) was consulted and a
group discussion was held to reach a consensus.

Statistical analysis
Post-operative outcomes were pooled as risk ratios (OR) with their
95% confidence intervals (CIs). To help account for any heterogeneity between the included studies, the random effects model
was utilized. Pairwise meta-analysis was conducted in R, version
4.0.2 using the meta and metafor packages.22 Heterogeneity was
assessed using The Higgins’ and Thompson’s I2 statistic23 and
rated as low (I2 = 0–25%), moderate (I2 = 25–50%), or high
(I2 > 50%) (Figs. 1–6). Additionally, leave-one-out sensitivity
analyses were performed whenever moderate to severe heterogeneity was observed (Supplementary Fig. 3). A 2-tailed P < 0.05
was considered statistically significant for all analyses.
RESULTS
Study details
The comprehensive search strategy retrieved 1471 studies. After
applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 10 studies were
included in the final meta-analysis (Table 1, Supplementary
Table 4). At quality assessment, all reports received higher than
seven asterisks, indicating good quality.
A total of 892 pediatric patients were pooled, of whom 448
underwent topical anesthesia with EMLA cream and 473 underwent topical anesthesia with amethocaine gel. The number of
patients in each study ranged from 17 to 109, while the mean age
ranged from 4.8 to 8 years old. Females comprised between 29%
and 51% of the study populations.
Pair-wise meta-analysis
Details of pooled analyses for all outcomes can be found in
Figs. 1–6.
First attempt cannulation success rate
Compared to amethocaine gel, EMLA cream was associated with
lower rates of first cannulation success (RR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.97–0.99;
p = 0.017). No heterogeneity was seen between the studies
(I2 = 0%) (Fig. 1).
Child-reported absence of pain
Compared to amethocaine gel, EMLA cream was associated with
lower rates of child-reported absence of pain (RR, 0.58; 95% CI,
0.41–0.82; p = 0.011). Heterogeneity between the studies was low
(I2 = 20.2%) (Fig. 2).
Child-reported visual analogue scale (VAS) score
There were no significant differences between EMLA cream and
amethocaine gel for child-reported VAS (SMD, 0.43; 95% CI, −1.46
to 2.31; p = 0.525). There was high heterogeneity between the
studies (I2 = 92.8%) (Fig. 3). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses
confirmed the solidity of these findings (Supplementary Fig. 3).
Parent-reported visual analogue scale (VAS) score
There were no significant differences between EMLA cream and
amethocaine gel for parent-reported VAS (SMD, −0.05; 95% CI,
−0.64 to 0.54; p = 0.478). No heterogeneity was seen between the
studies (I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4).
Observed pain score
There were no significant differences between EMLA cream and
amethocaine gel for observed pain score (SMD, −0.45; 95% CI,
−2.27 to 1.38; p = 0.405). I2 was 88.6%, indicating high heterogeneity between the studies (Fig. 5). Leave-one-out sensitivity
analyses confirmed the solidity of these findings (Supplementary
Fig. 3.1).

DISCUSSION
The meta‐analyses from this systematic review confirm that
amethocaine gel is superior to EMLA cream for child-reported
absence of pain and first cannulation success. Presently, this
pairwise meta-analysis of 10 studies pooling over 890 pediatric
patients undergoing topical anesthesia with EMLA cream or
amethocaine gel is the first and most comprehensive review on
the topic. Compared to amethocaine gel, topical anesthetic use of
EMLA cream was associated with lower rates of child-reported
absence of pain and first cannulation success. There were no
differences seen between EMLA cream and amethocaine gel for
child-reported acceptable anesthesia or observed pain score.
Additionally, no differences were associated with child- or parentreported VAS within the EMLA or amethocaine-treated groups.
Topical anesthetics are recommended for the treatment of
nonemergent painful procedures, such as laceration repair,
venipuncture, incisions, and drainage, according to the American
Academy of Pediatrics (AAP).1,24 Despite their slow onset, topical
anesthetics are not indicated for emergent local anesthesia, as
they reduce post-infiltration pain. In areas with rich blood supply,
they are highly effective but less effective in areas with a limited

subcutaneous vascular network.24 According to guidelines, EMLA
cream should be applied to intact skin and covered with an
occlusive dressing (e.g., Tegaderm) for 60 min after application.
Anesthesia onset occurs 60 min after application and peaks at
120 min, lasting between 1 and 2 h after removal, although 4 h has
also been reported. In infants and children, 3 months or older
weighing over 5 kg, 1−2 g of EMLA cream should be applied per
10 cm2 of skin. Prilocaine in the formulation causes mild, transient
skin irritation and methemoglobinemia, two adverse reactions
associated with EMLA cream. For this reason, it is avoided in
infants 12 months of age and younger.25
The alternative, amethocaine gel, is commonly used outside of
the United States. Guidelines suggest that 1 g per 6.25 cm2 of skin
should be applied and covered with an occlusive dressing (e.g.,
gauze with tape or adhesive polyurethane film dressing [eg,
Tegaderm or Opsite]) for 30 to 45 min. The duration of action for
amethocaine gel is typically 4–6 h. In addition to assisting in
venous cannulation, skin erythema is a normal consequence of
tetracaine-induced capillary dilation.25 Of note, the trials included
in this study’s cohort are in line with the suggested guidelines of
topical anesthetic application and duration time but differ from
one another.
Similarly, in a 2017 meta-analysis of 3 RCTs, Pywell et al. issued a
weak recommendation for the use of amethocaine in the pediatric
cannulation when compared with EMLA.26 A key difference

success rates.
Taddio et al. performed a systematic review in 2001 composed
of 8 studies comparing lidocaine–prilocaine with tetracaine in
children for 4 different procedures: intravenous cannulation,
venipuncture, Port-a-Cath puncture, and laser therapy.27
Lidocaine-prilocaine and tetracaine appeared to be equally
effective for procedural pain relief when used according to
instructions. Tetracaine, however, is more effective when applied
for the same period of time as lidocaine-prilocaine. In this study, a
pairwise meta-analysis was conducted of a pooled cohort to build
upon these findings. In pediatric patients undergoing topical
anesthesia during a wide array of skin-related procedures, there is
a scarcity of randomized control trials assessing the efficacy of
EMLA cream and amethocaine gel.
This pairwise meta-analysis must be interpreted in the context
of its limitations with respect to pain assessment. The reliance on
child-reported, visual analogue, and observational pain scores,
devoid of a universally accepted rating scale, introduced a
limitation in the precision and comparability of the collected
data. The use of observation-based pain scores, while providing
valuable insights, inherently lacks the objectivity afforded by
standardized rating scales. This limitation is particularly pertinent
in pediatric populations, where subjective factors such as age and
sex may have undue influence, compromising the reliability and
accuracy of pain assessments. Child-reported pain scales themselves exhibit variability, as factors such as developmental
differences, cognitive abilities, and individual perceptions contribute to the nuanced interpretation of pain by pediatric subjects.
Furthermore, many of the trials employed measurement tools that
were not adequately established or evaluated through psychometric testing. This could potentially undermine the validity and
reliability of the findings obtained from those studies. Therefore, it
is crucial to ensure that the scales used in research are rigorously
tested to ensure their accuracy and precision. The subjective
nature of pain and its measurement can be attributed to its lack in
clinical research. The need for a standardized tool to measure the
impact of these topical anesthetics on pain is evident for both

pediatric and adult populations, and future research endeavors
should continue to address this.
Additionally, variable application methods and durations of the
two topical anesthetics, amethocaine gel (30 to 45 min) and EMLA
cream (over 60 min), posed a limitation to this study. The disparate
timeframes for application arise from the distinct chemical properties of these anesthetics, posing a challenge in achieving a uniform
approach. This lack of standardization in application duration
introduces a potential confounding factor that requires consideration when interpreting the results of this study, particularly in the
context of comparing the efficacy and outcomes between
amethocaine gel and EMLA cream. In turn, having topical
anesthetics applied for different durations can result in bias,
especially if participants know the implications and the data
collectors know the duration. Due to differences in cream amounts
and application durations, a double-blinded comparison could not
be performed among the included trials. Making the cream
amounts equal would have a negative impact on the concentration
of active ingredients and ultimately diminish its effectiveness.
In interpreting the evidence presented in this meta-analysis, a
number of limitations must be considered. First, due to a lack of
randomized trials comparing amethocaine gel and EMLA cream,
this analysis was limited to observational studies and any
subsequent confounder or treatment allocation bias. Second, this
pooled analysis included data from multiple centers, each
presenting unique operator experiences with topical anesthesia
in pediatric populations and learning curves that may have
influenced outcomes. However, these differences in center quality
and expertise come with the benefit of providing insight into
topical anesthesia in a “real-world” clinical setting. Third, this
analysis revealed no significant differences between EMLA cream
and amethocaine gel in child-observed VAS score and observed
pain score. Of note, there is a high degree of heterogeneity
between the two groups. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were
performed in an effort to control for this, which confirmed the
solidity of this study’s results. Additionally, the random effects
model was used to account for heterogeneity between studies
and more conservatively estimate effect sizes. However, in the
absence of high evidence randomized control trials, the hope is
that this review will help guide clinicians as they decide the
optimal treatment approach for their patients.

CONCLUSION
Amethocaine gel is superior to EMLA cream for child-reported
absence of pain and first cannulation success. There were no
differences seen between EMLA cream and amethocaine gel for
child-reported acceptable anesthesia or observed pain score.
Additionally, no differences were associated with child- or parentreported VAS within the EMLA or amethocaine-treated groups.
These findings support the use of amethocaine gel in pediatric
patients, although large, randomized trials comparing EMLA
cream and amethocaine gel in pediatric patients are still needed.

AUTHOR CONTRIBUTIONS
Two authors (K.V. & A.A.) reviewed 1438 titles and abstracts after eliminating duplicate
citations. Two investigators (A.A. & D.M.) reviewed the full texts of the remaining articles
for eligibility, with disagreements being resolved by a third investigator (P.F.). After
reviewing the articles, two authors (K.V. and A.A.) independently extracted data from
the studies that were included. If there were any disagreements, a third author (M.A.)
reviewed them. To ensure accuracy and fairness, two separate authors (A.A. & D.M.)
evaluated the quality of each study using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale for observational
studies, which rates observational studies quality via asterisk score in three domains: up
to four asterisks for patient selection, up to two for comparability of study arms, and up
to three asterisks for sufficient outcome follow-up duration/assessment. In cases where
there were discrepancies, a third author (P.F.) was consulted and a group discussion was
held to reach a consensus. One author (M.A.) oversaw the editing and development of
the manuscript.
