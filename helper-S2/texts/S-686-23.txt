The Safety of Perioperative Liposomal Bupivacaine
Injections in Dermatologic Surgery Patients
Melissa M. Rames, MD,* Jorge A. Rios-Duarte, MD,* and Nahid Y. Vidal, MD†

BACKGROUND There is a paucity of literature investigating the use of liposomal bupivacaine (LB) in a dermatologic
surgery setting.
OBJECTIVE To identify the treatment characteristics and adverse events, including Local Anesthetic System Toxicity
(LAST), in patients receiving perioperative LB.
MATERIALS AND METHODS A retrospective chart review was performed at a single institution using an institutional
database and key words “Exparel” or “liposomal bupivacaine.” Patients $18 years old who received perioperative LB to
the surgical site and surrounding skin were included.
RESULTS A total of 140 LB administrations and 129 patients were included. Liposomal bupivacaine was used most often
in the setting of excisions (n 5 88, 62.9%) and Mohs micrographic surgery (n 5 49, 35%), with hidradenitis suppurativa
(n 5 77, 55%) and cutaneous neoplasms (n 5 56, 40%) the most frequent diagnoses. The median postoperative area and
length were 23.6 cm2 and 15.4 cm, respectively. The median LB dosage was 20 mL (range 1.5–20). There were no adverse
events in 134 out of 136 patients of LB administration (98.5%). There were 2 reports (1.5%) of nausea on POD1 while
these patients were taking opioids.
CONCLUSION Liposomal bupivacaine should be considered for postoperative analgesia in patients undergoing dermatologic surgery because it is well tolerated and efficacious. The authors observed no cases of LAST.

that provides sustained release analgesia for 72 hours3 and
may be a promising option in this setting. However, its use
in dermatologic surgery is off-label and its safety profile is
largely understudied.
The primary concern with LB is the potential risk of
Local Anesthetics System Toxicity (LAST), particularly
with high cumulative dosing or inadvertent intravascular
injections.5–7 The risk of LAST is dose dependent and
includes both central nervous system (CNS) and cardiovascular system (CVS) toxicity.5,6 Central nervous system

Event Reporting System (FAERS) demonstrated low incidences overall but did not specify patient population or

used in conjunction with other local anesthetics, there is a
theoretical concern that interactions with these anesthetics
may result in displacement and release of free bupivacaine
from liposomes into the bloodstream.14

is to wait 20 minutes between injection of other local
anesthetics and LB, and to allow 96 hours between injection

adverse events have not been studied in dermatologic
surgery, but there is extensive evidence of LB safety and
tolerability in patients undergoing general, thoracic, plastic,
or orthopedic surgeries.8,16,17
Outside of LAST, the most common adverse events (AEs)
for LB in clinical trials were nausea, constipation, and
vomiting ($10%).13 These studies included local infiltration for bunionectomy or hemorrhoidectomy (dose range
66–532 mg) or nerve block studies (dose of 133 mg or 266
mg) used in various surgical procedures.13 The aim of this
retrospective study is to evaluate the safety of LB when used
in patients undergoing outpatient dermatologic surgeries.

Materials and Methods
The study was institutional review board approved. A
retrospective chart review was performed at a single
institution. Electronic health records were queried using
key search terms including “Exparel,” “liposomal bupivacaine” and screened for use during dermatologic patient
encounters. Patients $18 years old between October 2015
through October 2024 who received perioperative LB
(Exparel) at the surgical site perioperatively in dermatologic
surgery were included. Patients ,18 years old or those who
received LB from other specialties were excluded. Owing to
the retrospective nature of this study, adverse events were
identified through chart review, and the absence of reported
LAST cases was inferred from lack of documentation.
Statistics were calculated using R version 4.4.1. Patient
and case characteristics were summarized using descriptive
statistics. Numeric variables were described using mean/SD
or median/range (as appropriate). Categorical variables
were described as frequencies and proportions. To compare
case characteristics between patients with and without
reported postoperative pain, a Wilcoxon rank-sum test at p
value , .05 significance was used.

Results
A total of 136 occurrences of liposomal bupivacaine (13.3
mg/mL) administration in 127 patients were included in the
study. There were no adverse events in 134 out of 136 cases
of LB administration (98.5%). There were 2 reports (1.5%)
of nausea on Postoperative day 1 (POD1) while these
patients were taking opioids (tramadol 50 mg every 6 hours
as needed).
In total, 52% were male patients, and the median age was
48.6 years. Liposomal bupivacaine was used most often in
the setting of excisions (n 5 85, 62.5%) followed by MMS
(n 5 48, 35.3%). Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) was the
most frequent diagnosis (n 5 74, 54.4%) associated with
the procedure where LB was administered, followed by
cutaneous neoplasms (n 5 56, 41.2%)—most commonly
squamous cell carcinoma (16/56, 28.6%). The axillae
(n 5 39, 28.7%), groin (n 5 36, 26.5%), anogenital
(n 5 27, 19.9%), face (n 5 21, 15.4%), and scalp and neck
(n 5 17, 12.5%) regions were the most frequent anatomic
sites.

The most common repair type associated with LB
administration was a partial or guided closure (n 5 55,
39.3%), secondary intention (n 5 52, 38.2%), and
intermediate layered closure (n 5 33, 24.3%). Twenty
cases (14.7%) had referred closures with other surgical
specialties (but received LB with dermatology), 16 (11.8%)
underwent complex layered closure, and 2 had flaps
(1.5%). There was 1 simple closure (0.7%). The median
LB dosage was 20 mL corresponding to 266 mg LB (range
1.5–20 mL, 20.4–266 mg). The median postoperative area
and length were 23.6 cm2 (0.3–871 cm2) and 15.5 cm
(1.6–70 cm), respectively. The median number of operative
sites per encounter was 1.0, although maximum was as high
as 22 surgical sites (1.0–22.0). Out of the 136 occurrences of
LB administration in 127 patients, there was 40 (29.4%)
procedures associated with a prescription for oral opioids to
be taken as needed (PRN) for severe acute pain. A detailed
description of case characteristics associated with LB
administrations is given in Table 1.

Discussion
This study demonstrates that LB is a well-tolerated and safe
option for postoperative analgesia in patients undergoing
dermatologic surgery, with no adverse events in 98.5% of
LB administrations. Importantly, there were no CNS or
CVS AEs in this cohort. Only 2 patients (1.5%) (each with 1
LB administration) reported nausea on POD1, likely related
to concomitant oral opioids per documented clinical
suspicion. This study contributes to the limited data on LB
use in dermatologic surgery.
The 1 adverse event in this cohort was nausea (n 5 2,
1.5%), which is consistent with clinical trials demonstrating
nausea as one of the most frequently reported adverse events
after LB administration.18 An analysis looking at 10 studies
in which LB was used in setting of hemorrhoidectomy,
bunionectomy, breast augmentation, total knee arthroplasty, or hernia repair reported that most AEs were
classified as mild (.90%), the most common being nausea
(3.3%), constipation (2.0%), and vomiting (1.3%); the 2
patients in this study with nausea were also taking
postoperative opioids.8 Of note, nausea still had an
exceptionally low rate (3.3%).8 Adverse events were
generally higher in those with higher doses of LB (.266
mg), in patients $65 years old, and in patients with preexisting comorbidities.8 Four patients experienced
treatment-related bradycardia and 1 patient experienced
treatment-related tachycardia, none of which required
intervention.8 Liposomal bupivacaine does not necessitate
continuous cardiac monitoring and, therefore, the authors
do not monitor the patients with active heart rate (HR)
monitors at this institution, although the authors do
measure pre- and post-LB administration HR and assess
patient status clinically. Patients are observed for 15 to 30
minutes after injection and discharged with written and
verbal instructions to monitor for signs up to 96 hours after
injection.

popliteal fossa, and adductor canal blocks in adults), the
maximum recommended dose is 133 mg per block.18 The
safety and efficacy of LB for other regional blocks have not
been established or officially recommended by the FDA.18
Assuming the same dose in milligrams injected, the risk of
LAST may be higher with regional nerve blocks using LB
compared with local infiltration, primarily because of
prolonged systemic absorption and higher vascularity with
nerve blocks and, therefore, a risk of inadvertent vascular
injection.5,6,18 However, there are limited data directly
quantifying the difference in LAST between regional nerve
blocks and local infiltration with LB.
Despite these theoretical concerns, studies investigating
the incidence of LAST with LB are sparse. Dagenais and
colleagues13 used the FAERS to identify reports of LAST
using 3 different approaches/definitions, though doses and
clinical scenarios were not specified. Overall, the incidence
of LAST was low, reported as 0.22% (29/12,854), 0.09%
(75/76,762), or 0.05% (42/91,181) cases of LAST out of all
reported adverse events with any injectable anesthetic,
depending on the analytic approach.
The route of administration also affects both the onset
and duration of analgesia with LB. Local infiltration
generally produces faster and more predicable analgesia,
with some studies reporting clinically meaningful analgesia
within 2 minutes and substantial pain relief by 5 minutes
after local infiltration.19 In contrast, nerve blocks with LB
may have a less predictable and slower onset. A study
investigating ulnar nerve blocks reported success in 32%
after use of LB compared with 100% of patients who had
plain bupivacaine with a significantly delayed time to onset
with LB.20,21 Similarly, a study on sciatic nerve blocks
reported a median time of onset of 18 to 29 minutes.21
Duration of analgesia also varies by route, with regional
nerve blocks providing longer lasting pain control than

plasma levels of LB can persist for up to 96 hours after

dermatologic surgery-specific settings.
In general, the authors recommend nerve blocks on the
face using plain bupivacaine because of its faster onset19,20,23 and increased risk of potential vascular absorption of injectables on the face. Although LB is approved for
regional nerve blocks, there are limited data guiding optimal
volumes for facial nerve blocks as opposed to local
infiltration. If LB is used for a facial nerve block, only the
minimum effective volume should be administered, taking
into account the necessity for extended (721 hour)
anesthesia. On the face, excess volume can lead to nerve
irritation, systemic absorption, edema, and unintentional
anesthetic spread. Owing to the anatomic constraints of
facial planes, less LB is needed—just enough to bathe the
nerve. At this institution, nerve blocks are performed per
surgeon discretion, either with lidocaine mixed with plain
bupivacaine or with plain bupivacaine.
For initial local anesthesia at this institution, lidocaine or
buffered lidocaine is used, with LB reserved for after
completion of complex or extensive procedures. When
using LB in flap cases, surgeons should perform their usual
local anesthesia for reconstruction and ensure meticulous
hemostasis, because the prolonged duration of action of LB
(up to 96 hours) increases the risk of adverse events if
additional anesthetic is later required. It is advisable to
ensure the procedure is complete before injecting LB,
allowing at least a 20-minute interval after the last local
anesthetic injection to reduce the risk of premature release
of bupivacaine in accordance with the manufacturer and
pharmacy recommendations.3,4,7 In this study, this was
typically after the completion of the procedure while the
patient was still under the effect of local anesthesia. For
cases performed in the operative room (e.g., HS cases), LB
was injected immediately after the case with the patient
under sedation or anesthesia.
Injections were performed as local infiltrations. For
linear closures and flaps, the authors recommend LB
infiltration using a total of 5 to 10 mL, 0.5 to 1.0 mL per
1 cm of incision length. This is injected in subcutaneous
tissue around the flap base, pedicle, and inset margins. If
more volume is needed for a very large flap, this can be
diluted from 1:2 to 1:4 with normal saline, using a fanning

For patients who received LB with dermatologic surgery
and repairs with other specialties, the other specialty teams
were aware of LB administration—all patients receive a
bracelet after LB administration, and it is well documented
in the chart. General anesthesia is considered safe within the
72 hours post-LB administration, with caution advised
when using agents that may potentiate CNS or cardiac
toxicity. The authors recommend that irrespective of the
setting, patients should be discharged with an LB bracelet
(provided by the manufacturer) with documentation including exact administration time.
Limitations of this study include its retrospective nature,
small sample size, and single institution data. Owing to the
retrospective nature of this study, adverse events were
determined based on available documentation. The authors
believe this is sufficient, because most instances of LAST, if
they were to occur, would likely manifest within the timeline
of a typical MMS encounter (most commonly immediate,
but can be delayed up to 45 minutes or rarely longer)9 and
be thoroughly documented in accordance with the institution’s standards. Future prospective studies are necessary to
investigate the rate of adverse events across various
dermatologic procedures. In addition, studies with a control
arm are needed to evaluate the efficacy of LB versus placebo
while accounting for prescription oral analgesics.
Overall, LB is a well-tolerated option for postoperative
analgesia in patients undergoing dermatologic surgery and
may be considered. This study contributes to the limited
data on LB use in this setting. Although the authors had no
incidences of LAST (CNS or CVS AEs), it is important to
monitor for the aforementioned signs of toxicity when
administering LB and wait the recommend times between
other local anesthetic administration.5,6,8,18
