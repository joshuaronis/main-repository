Safety of peak serum lidocaine concentration after
Mohs micrographic surgery: A prospective
cohort study
Murad Alam, MD, MSCI,a,b,c Dominic Ricci, MD,e Jillian Havey, BS,a Alfred Rademaker, PhD,d
Joslyn Witherspoon, MD, MPH,a and Dennis P. West, PhDa
Chicago and Maywood, Illinois
Background: Large volumes of dilute local anesthesia are increasingly used not only for liposuction but also
for other large cutaneous surgeries, including skin cancer excision. Although the lidocaine serum levels and
peaks after injection of tumescent anesthesia in the trunk were shown to be safe even when used in high doses,
the levels after use of less dilute (1% lidocaine) solutions for facial cancer surgery have not been described.
Objective: We sought to ascertain whether perioperative peak lidocaine levels during Mohs micrographic
surgery approach thresholds for lidocaine toxicity.
Methods: In this prospective cohort study, each Mohs micrographic procedure was commenced per
routine protocol, with use of injectable 1% lidocaine with 1:200,000 epinephrine and 1:10 8.4% bicarbonate
for local anesthesia. Six peripheral blood draws of 5 mL each were performed and sent for serum lidocaine
concentration measurement. Blood draws were obtained before and after the first two stages of Mohs
micrographic surgery, and before and after the third stage or wound repair, as appropriate. Subjects and
investigators reported any symptoms or signs of lidocaine toxicity. The main outcome measures include:
(1) number of subjects exhibiting at least one level higher than the threshold for toxicity; and (2) sustained
elevated lidocaine levels in one or more subjects.
Results: Mean total lidocaine volume per subject was 15 mL (range: 5-48 mL). The highest peak serum
lidocaine level detected at any time point (blood draw) for any subject was 0.3 g/mL. Mean serum
lidocaine level for all 6 time points was less than 0.1 g/mL and was thus not detected. Detectable lidocaine
levels were found to be associated with higher mean (P = .028) and median (P = .008) total lidocaine doses.
Because no toxic levels were detected, it was not possible to measure sustained high lidocaine levels.
Limitations: Limitations of this study include the lack of use of very concentrated (eg, 2% vs 1%) lidocaine
solution.
Conclusions: Lidocaine use during Mohs micrographic surgery does not appear to result in serum
lidocaine levels approaching toxic levels even when relatively high total lidocaine doses are used. There is
an association between higher total lidocaine dose and perioperative peak serum lidocaine level. ( J Am
Acad Dermatol 2010;63:87-92.)
Key words: local anesthesia; Mohs micrographic; safety; surgery; toxicity.

After completion of the blood draws, the intravenous line was carefully removed and discarded.
To assess lidocaine levels over time, blood draws
were collected over a maximum of 8 hours. Patients
were queried by investigator-administered questionnaire regarding any subjectively noted symptoms (dizziness, drowsiness, or lightheadedness;
muscle twitching, ringing of the ears, blurred or
double vision, or numbness/tingling in places not
around the injection site; sensations of warmth or
cold; and chest pain or back pain) associated with
early lidocaine toxicity. Investigators assessed any
signs of lidocaine toxicity by serial physical
examination during the course of the surgery.
Specifically, patients were monitored for slow or
shallow breathing (signs of respiratory depression);
drowsiness or dizziness (associated with bradycardia and hypotension); nervousness, euphoria, confusion, tremors, and twitching (signs of central
nervous system manifestations, which can be excitatory and/or depressant); and seizures or loss of
consciousness (indications of severe neurologic
and/or circulatory impairment).

Outcomes and follow-up
As this study had a single screening and intervention visit, there was no follow-up beyond the 6 hours
of the study procedure. The primary outcome measure was peak lidocaine level per subject, as detected
by the series of 6 blood draws. The secondary
outcome measure was subjective (patient self-report
via investigator-administered questionnaire) or

objective (investigator detected) assessment of signs
and symptoms of lidocaine toxicity.
Statistical analysis
The primary objective of this study was to determine whether local injection of lidocaine used during Mohs micrographic surgery will elevate serum
lidocaine concentrations to a level that could cause
systemic symptoms. This was assessed by descriptive
statistics, with counting of the number of enrolled
patients exhibiting levels higher than the threshold
for toxicity. Because the aim was to detect sustained
elevated lidocaine levels, such an adverse outcome
was defined as 3 or more toxic lidocaine levels out of
the 6 measured (either contiguous or sporadic) for
any specific subject. With 20 patients, this study
provided a 95% chance of detecting lidocaine toxicity if the underlying true rate of toxicity in this
population were 12% or greater.
In addition, subjects were subdivided into two
categories, those with all lidocaine levels undetectable (\0.1 g/mL), and those with at least one level
that was detectable ([0.1 g/mL). For the two
groups, the median (rank sum test) and mean (t
test) total lidocaine doses per subject were compared, as were the mean (t test) ages of subjects in
the two groups.

hypothesis that systemic absorption of lidocaine
from the head and neck is more efficient than that
from the trunk, and therefore potentially more likely
to create harmfully high peak lidocaine levels, was
thus not supported. Similarly, it did not appear that
the higher concentration of Mohs micrographic surgery anesthesia compared with tumescent anesthesia predisposed patients to higher or more rapidly
elevated peak lidocaine levels.
Investigators did not note any symptoms or signs
of lidocaine toxicity in any of the subjects. The two
subjects reporting ‘‘headache’’ and ‘‘feeling shaky,’’
respectively, did so after the first stage of Mohs
micrographic surgery, when this was more likely
associated with epinephrine-induced tachycardia
than lidocaine effect; even at their sixth and final
blood draw, neither of these subjects had lidocaine
levels above the 0.1-g/mL level.
Significantly, this study specifically obtained serial
lidocaine levels over a span of up to 8 hours
(maximum 7.8 hours, mean 4.4 hours). Although
the precise blood collection times varied because of
the constraints of Mohs micrographic surgery, this
overall target permitted assessment of lidocaine
levels at the 5.8-hour point that Rubin et al9 reported
being associated with peak levels when injections
are above the level of the clavicles.
Surprisingly, even high-volume total doses of as
much as 48 mL of 1% lidocaine with epinephrine and
bicarbonate were associated with lidocaine levels of
no more than 0.3 g/mL, a tenth the level associated
with the earliest and mildest symptoms of lidocaine
toxicity. For the sake of comparison, the quantity of
lidocaine in 50 mL of 1% lidocaine is comparable
with that in the volume of tumescent fluid found by
Ostad et al8 to be safe during body liposuction in an
average-sized (70 kg) adult. Based on our data, it
appears that significantly more than 50 mL of 1%
lidocaine can be used safely in Mohs micrographic
surgery performed over several hours.
There were some limitations in the study data.
First, serum rather than plasma lidocaine levels were
measured. Depending on the substance for which a
blood level is being measured, serum and plasma
levels may be similar, serum levels may be higher, or
plasma levels may be higher. For lidocaine levels,
plasma and serum levels are similar, and serum levels
are more routinely reported by reference laboratories. Hence our selection of serum levels for this
study.
Our results showed a peak serum level 10 to
30 times below the plasma toxicity threshold and
this huge difference is highly unlikely to be
accounted for by minor differences in measurement technique.

Another limitation is that blood was not drawn
from subjects many hours after their final lidocaine
injection as this was impractical and not in patients’
best medical interest. It is thus theoretically possible
that lidocaine levels would have continued to
increase for some time after the last blood draw
before peaking. However, it is also likely the contribution of the initial lidocaine injections would start to
wane as the contribution of the final injections would
increase.
This study does add to the information currently
provided by the Food and Drug Administration
(FDA) regarding clinical use of lidocaine.
According to the package insert,10 during epidural
anesthesia, peripheral nerve block, and local infiltration, the recommended maximum dosage of
lidocaine hydrochloride and epinephrine injection,
United States Pharmacopeia, should not exceed
7 mg/kg (3.5 mg/lb) of body weight, and in general
it is recommended that the maximum total dose
should not exceed 500 mg. These FDA recommendations apply to total dose regardless of route of
administration. There are also less stringent recommendations that serve only as a guide: among these
are the suggestion that percutaneous infiltration be
limited to 1 to 60 mL of 0.5% to 1% lidocaine, with a
total dose of 300 mg. Very significantly, the total dose
recommendations appear to apply only to the dose
delivered at one point in time. Thus, it is noted that
the half-life of lidocaine during epidural use is 1.5 to
2 hours, and it is therefore deemed allowable to
re-dose at this site after 90 minutes. Similar considerations presumably apply to intradermal, subcutaneous, and percutaneous injections, but specific time
intervals for re-dosing are not given for these. Our
study provides further information about the interval
dosing of lidocaine at head and neck sites, and
shows that, as in the case of epidural anesthesia, redosing can be safely performed when doses infiltrated at any one time point do not exceed a
threshold amount. It is noteworthy that 3 of our 20
patients received total doses in excess of the 300 mg
that FDA recommends for skin infiltration, but as
these total doses were delivered over a span of many
hours, no adverse events occurred.
Overall, the results of this study suggest that
moderate-volume, repeated injections of 1% lidocaine during Mohs micrographic surgery of the head
and neck are safe. Although this study does not
provide information regarding a safe maximum total
dose, it strongly suggests that at least 50 mL of 1%
lidocaine and likely much more can be used in the
staged Mohs micrographic procedure without any
adverse signs or symptoms, and with barely detectable systemic serum lidocaine levels.
